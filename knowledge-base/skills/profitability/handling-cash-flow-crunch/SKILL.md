# Handling a Cash Flow Crunch

*You opened the bank account, saw payroll on Friday, and felt your stomach drop. That moment is what this is for — you have more levers than panic suggests, but you have to pull them in order.*

## When this triggers

Pull this skill the moment any of these are true:

- Owner asks "will I make payroll", "cash is tight", "we're running out", or names a Friday.
- Cash balance is under one payroll cycle and the next payroll is within 14 days.
- Overdue A/R (30+ days) exceeds the next two pay periods — the classic slow-pay crunch.
- Net cash projection is negative inside 30 days with no signed bridge.
- Owner is about to drain personal savings or sign on a high-cost capital instrument.

Before pulling any lever, name the crunch type out loud — slow-pay (A/R problem), high-overhead (cost problem), revenue-gap (sales problem), or growth-burn (intentional, mistimed). Pulling the wrong lever — a line of credit on a sales gap — buys 60 days and a bigger hole.

## What to do

The order matters. Lever 1 is cheapest because it's money that's already yours. Lever 2 is next because vendors are renegotiable. Lever 3 costs the most and takes the longest — never lead with it.

### Lever 1 — Accelerate AR (Day 1, today)

This is money you've already earned. The biggest determinant of recovery is whether you actually send the emails on time. Agencies that dedicate time each week to A/R recover 20% of overdue balances in the first two weeks.

**1a. Run the touch-3 cadence on every overdue invoice, today.**

| Overdue age | Touch | Decision criterion |
|---|---|---|
| 7 days past due | Friendly reminder email | Send to every invoice in this bucket today |
| 14 days past due | Firm email + mention 1.5%/mo late fee | Add the fee to the invoice |
| 30 days past due | Stop-work warning + offer call | Pause delivery if no response in 7 days |
| 45 days past due | Stop-work notice in writing | Work pauses until current |
| 60+ days past due | Final notice → collections handoff at 7 days no response | Recovery rate drops below 50% past 90 days |

Send all three older buckets before close of business today. Agencies wait too long out of guilt — by the time they hand off, it's uncollectible.

**1b. Offer 2/10 net 30 to your largest A/R balances.**

"2% off if paid within 10 days, full amount due in 30." On a $10,000 invoice, the client pays $9,800 on Day 10 instead of $10,000 on Day 30. The annualized cost is ~36.7% APR, but in a crunch you are buying days, not optimizing cost of capital. Early-payment discounts accelerate payment by 10–15 days on average.

Offer it tactically — to the top 3–5 A/R balances during the crunch, not as a permanent price cut.

**1c. Switch every new contract to upfront billing, starting today.**

- 50% upfront, 50% on delivery for project work.
- Month-in-advance retainer (not arrears) for ongoing work.
- ACH autopay required for any retainer over $5,000/month.

Top performers keep DSO at 30–45 days by structuring billing this way, not by chasing harder.

**1d. Send to collections at 60+ days with no payment plan.**

Collections agencies charge 25–50% contingency. Half of something beats all of nothing. Do not wait past 90 days — recovery rate collapses.

### Lever 2 — Slow AP (Day 1–3)

You are buying days, not skipping payments. The U.S. Chamber rule: reach out 30 days *before* the bill is due, while you still have credibility. Never just stop paying without a conversation.

**2a. Triage every vendor into three buckets, today.**

| Bucket | Examples | Action |
|---|---|---|
| Critical | Hosting, core SaaS, payroll provider, key contractors | Transparency call + specific ask (see script below) |
| Important, replaceable | Marketing tools, secondary SaaS | Downgrade or cancel this week |
| Non-essential | Anything you'd be embarrassed to admit you pay for | Cancel today |

The 90-day usage test on subscriptions: used in last 30 days → keep; 1–3 times in 90 days → downgrade; untouched in 90 days → cancel. A typical 10-person agency finds $1,500–$4,000/month here.

**2b. For critical vendors, ask in this order of preference.**

1. Extended terms — net 15 → net 60 for three months.
2. Payment plan on the existing balance — e.g. $24,000 split over 6 months at $4,000.
3. Skip + extension — skip June, resume July, add the skipped amount to the back end.

Get the agreement in writing — an email confirming is enough; a signed addendum is better. A verbal modification neither side remembers in 60 days is worse than no deal.

**2c. Fix contractor payment terms now.**

The standard agency mistake: paying contractors net 15 while collecting net 45 — you are financing clients with contractor money. New agreements default to net 30, paid when client pays. For existing contractors, have the conversation. Most prefer "reliable net 30" over "promised net 15, actual net 22."

**2d. Freeze non-essential hiring this week.**

Pause open reqs. Candidates mid-interview: "We're pausing for 60 days. Can we revisit?" The cost of an early-tenure hire — recruiting, onboarding, ramp — is $30–60K in 90 days. That's runway.

**Do NOT delay:** payroll, payroll tax, sales tax, anything personally guaranteed, anything with a 5-day grace before service shutoff (hosting, payments processor), or anything that cuts off client delivery. Slowing these turns a 60-day cash problem into a 90-day existential one.

### Lever 3 — Raise short-term capital (Day 3 → Week 6)

Last resort. Three out of four crunches resolve with Levers 1 and 2 alone. Pull this only when the 60-day forecast still shows a shortfall after the first two levers are in motion.

| Source | Cost (APR) | Speed | When it fits |
|---|---|---|---|
| Line of credit | 8–18% | 2–6 weeks | Predictable gap, established credit. Open it BEFORE you need it. |
| Invoice factoring | Effective 30–60% APR | 3–7 days | A/R-heavy, weaker credit, cash needed this week |
| Personal loan / HELOC | 7–15% | 1–3 weeks | Founder-bridge, small amount, genuinely temporary problem |
| Equity (F&F / angel) | Permanent dilution | 30–90 days | Structural reset, not bridge |

**Decision criteria, in order:**

1. **Line of credit** — cheapest legitimate option, interest only on what you draw. The catch: 2–6 weeks to close, and the bank will see your tight position. The right time to open one is before the crunch is visible.
2. **Invoice factoring** — fits when you can't get a line of credit and need cash this week. Factor advances 80–90% on the invoice within days. Anything past 60–90 days of continuous factoring signals a structural problem, not a bridge.
3. **HELOC at 8%** is cheaper than factoring at effective 30–60%. Use only if (a) the business problem is genuinely temporary and (b) the amount is small relative to your personal net worth.
4. **Never** drain personal emergency funds for business payroll. If the business fails anyway, you have neither business nor personal runway. Use credit, not cash.
5. **Equity raised during a visible crunch is the most expensive capital you'll take.** Valuations are punitive when you're a forced seller. Raise before the crunch is visible — or after you've stabilized.

## Conversation script

Two emails. Use verbatim. Edit bracketed fields. Don't soften.

### Email to a client offering early-pay discount (Lever 1)

> **Subject:** Invoice #[N] — early-pay option
>
> Hi [Name],
>
> Quick offer that might work for both of us. Invoice #[N] for $[amount] is due [date]. If you can process payment within the next 10 business days — by [date + 10 business days] — we'll apply a 2% discount, bringing the total to $[amount × 0.98].
>
> Updated invoice attached with the discount applied. ACH and wire details on page 2. If 10 days isn't workable, the original terms stand and no action needed on your side.
>
> Either way, let me know what to expect so I can update our forecast.
>
> Thanks, [Your name]

### Email to a vendor asking for net-60 extension (Lever 2)

> **Subject:** Quick ask on payment terms — [your company]
>
> Hi [Name],
>
> Want to be straight with you. We've been a customer for [X years/months] and the relationship matters. We're working through a 60-day cash flow squeeze — nothing existential, but real. I'm reaching out before our next invoice is due because I'd rather plan together than have you chasing me in 30 days.
>
> The ask: can we move from net [current] to net 60 for the next three invoices — [Month 1, Month 2, Month 3]? After that, back to standard terms. To make this work on your side, I can offer [one of: a longer service commitment / wire instead of ACH / pay 50% on time and 50% in 30 days / a small late fee on the extension].
>
> Happy to jump on a 10-minute call this week. Reply with a time that works, or let me know if there's someone on your finance team I should loop in directly.
>
> Thanks, [Your name]

If they say no, ask the follow-up: "What's the most flexibility you can give, even if it's not what I asked?" Most vendors have more discretion than their AR rep admits.

## What NOT to do

- **Don't pull the wrong lever.** A line of credit on a sales-gap crunch is a 60-day delay, not a fix. Diagnose first — slow-pay, high-overhead, revenue-gap, or growth-burn — then choose the lever.
- **Don't drain personal emergency funds for business payroll.** It feels noble. It isn't. Use business credit, not personal cash.
- **Don't lead with mass layoffs.** Severance + lost productivity + re-hire cost + brand damage is real money. Hiring freeze and contractor wind-down come before headcount cuts.
- **Don't hide from the team.** They sense it before you tell them. Closed doors and vague reassurances push top performers to start interviewing. Transparency with a specific plan beats silence — name the problem, name the plan, name the next update date.
- **Don't ignore A/R out of guilt.** "I don't want to chase Client X — they're a great client." Great clients pay on time. A client 60 days overdue isn't being great to you.
