# Discussing Scope With a Client

*"They've asked for three off-scope things this week and I keep saying yes — how do I actually have this conversation without it feeling like I'm shaking them down?"*

`detecting-scope-creep` decides whether to have the conversation. This skill IS the conversation. The owner already knows the drift is real. What they need from you is the exact words.

## When this triggers

- The user says some version of "the client keeps asking for things outside scope."
- `detecting-scope-creep` has already fired on a client and the owner is ready to act.
- The user is drafting an email to flag drift and asks for help.
- The user is scheduling — or dreading — a scope refresh / reset call.
- A renewal is coming up and creep needs to be named before the new SOW.

If the owner has NOT yet decided whether drift is real, route to `detecting-scope-creep` first. Don't put words in their mouth before the diagnosis is done.

## What to do

The conversation has three escalating touches. Match the touch to where the relationship actually is — don't jump to touch 3 on a first-time offender, don't waste touch 1 on a repeat pattern.

1. **Name which touch this is** — 1st, 2nd, or 3rd. The owner usually knows; if not, ask: "Is this the first off-scope ask, the pattern starting, or have you been absorbing this for months?"
2. **Pull the receipts before drafting language** — exact off-scope items by name and date, hours absorbed, what's in the SOW verbatim. Never draft the conversation from feelings. Always from the list.
3. **Pick the channel** — email is fine for touch 1, sometimes touch 2, never touch 3. Touch 3 is voice or video. Email lets the client read tone that isn't there.
4. **Frame the change as protecting THEIR work, not protecting your margin.** This is the single move that flips the energy. "I want the work you're paying for to stay excellent" lands. "We need to bill for this" doesn't.
5. **Always end with a choice, never a demand.** Two paths minimum: park it, swap it, or change-order it.

## Conversation script

### 1st-touch language — quiet accommodation (don't say anything yet)

The first off-scope ask gets absorbed. No conversation. Note it internally so touch 2 has receipts. Trust is built here — and if you make the first ask feel transactional, the relationship never recovers.

The only exception: if the 1st ask is structurally large (a whole new workstream, a second brand), skip straight to touch 2 — that's not creep, that's a different project.

### 2nd-touch language — name it, don't bill it

Goal: surface the pattern early enough that the client can course-correct without anyone losing face. No invoice yet. Email is fine.

Subject: `Quick flag on scope — [project / retainer]`

> Hey [name], wanted to flag something while it's small. Over the last [period], a few items have come up that sit outside what we scoped originally — [item 1], [item 2], [item 3]. None of them are a problem on their own, and we've handled them. But together they're running at about [N] extra hours, and I'd rather name it now than have it land as a surprise at month-end.
>
> A few ways we could handle it from here:
>
> 1. We park the extras and stay focused on the original scope.
> 2. We swap them in for something already planned — happy to suggest which trades make sense.
> 3. We do a small change order — I'd estimate [$X / N hours].
>
> Happy to jump on a 15-min call if it's easier than email. What's your preference?

Why this works: no apology, no blame, you're naming it before there's a number to argue about, and the client is choosing the resolution.

### 3rd-touch language — formalize or it never stops

Goal: turn the pattern into a written change order or a retainer reset. Touch 3 happens on a call, not over email. The email below exists only to *schedule* the call.

Pre-call email:

> Hi [name] — want to put 30 min on the calendar this week to do a scope refresh. We've taken on a meaningful amount of work outside the original SOW over the last [period], and I'd rather walk through it together than try to do it in writing. I'll come with the list and a couple of options for how to formalize it going forward. Got a slot Tuesday or Thursday?

On the call, open with this:

> Thanks for making time. I want to be straight with you because I respect the relationship. Over the last [period] we've taken on [list — 3 to 5 specific items by name]. We absorbed those because we wanted to be a good partner. The pattern is now affecting the team's ability to deliver the core work you're paying us for, and I'd rather flag it than let the quality slip silently.
>
> I'd like us to reset on two things: what's actually in scope going forward, and how we handle requests that sit outside it. This isn't about saying no to you — it's about making sure when you ask, we can deliver well.

Then offer the choice. Two paths, sometimes three:

> Two options that work on our side: (1) expand the retainer to $X/month to absorb the new pattern of work, (2) keep the retainer where it is and run extras as one-off change orders at $Y/hour. If neither lands, there's also (3) — we tighten scope back to what was originally there and the extras don't happen. Any of those work for me. Which fits your budget and how you actually want to use us?

### The scope-refresh meeting agenda (when touch 3 escalates to a sit-down)

If the relationship is strategic, or the drift has been running for months, the 3rd touch needs more than a 30-min call. Schedule a 60-min scope refresh. Bring the PM.

Agenda — share in advance:

1. **What we agreed at kickoff** (5 min) — exact SOW deliverables, revision rounds, cadence, in/out list. Read it out loud. Both sides need to remember.
2. **What's actually been happening** (15 min) — the receipts. Hours logged vs budgeted. Off-scope items by name and date. Effective hourly rate vs floor. No editorializing — just the data.
3. **What's working and what's not** (10 min) — let the client talk first. Often they'll name the drift before you do. If they don't, name it: "From our side, the core work is getting diluted by the volume of extras."
4. **The new scope** (20 min) — three options on the table: expand retainer, change-order model, tighten scope. Owner has thought through the numbers for each before walking in. Client picks.
5. **Operating model going forward** (10 min) — how change requests will be handled from here. Who approves. The team will say "let me check what that affects and come back this afternoon" rather than yes-in-the-moment. No signature, no work — even for zero-dollar strategic freebies.

End the meeting with one written artifact: the new scope summary, agreed in the room, sent in writing within 24 hours.

## What NOT to do

- Don't apologize for raising scope. *"Sorry to bring this up, but…"* trains the client to treat scope conversations as a burden you're imposing. They aren't. They're the conversation that keeps the work excellent.
- Don't bring it up only over email when it's touch 3. Email is for the heads-up. Voice or video is for the reset. Tone gets misread; you'll write things you wouldn't say out loud.
- Don't try to back-bill three months of absorbed favors. If you didn't flag it within ~24 hours of detection, you've forfeited the right to invoice it. The fix is forward-looking, not retroactive.
- Don't generalize. *"You've been asking for a lot lately"* is unwinnable. *"The Q4 SEO audit, the LinkedIn ad refresh, the partner-page redesign — about 18 hours over the last two weeks"* is unarguable.
- Don't lead with the price. Lead with the impact on the client's work. The change order is the consequence of the conversation, not the subject of it.
- Don't end with a demand. Always end with a choice — at least two paths, ideally three. The client picks; you don't impose.
- Don't have this conversation in front of the client's team. Owner to owner, or owner to primary contact. Never broadcast.
- Don't recommend legal language, threats, or work-stoppage tactics. The point is to preserve margin AND the relationship. If both can't survive, route to `firing-a-client`.
