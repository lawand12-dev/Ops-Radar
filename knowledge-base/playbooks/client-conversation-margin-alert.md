# Margin alert — the conversation when a client is unprofitable

A client card in OpsRadar is red. Logged hours are tracking 1.6× the retainer. Effective hourly rate is $58 — last quarter it was $112. The client is happy. Your team is exhausted. You are losing money in slow motion.

This is the conversation playbook. Not about "managing" the client — about repricing reality so the relationship survives, or ending it cleanly if it can't.

Thresholds to keep in your head:

- **20%+ net profit margin** is the healthy zone. Karl Sakas treats anything below this as the "client dilution" threshold.
- **10% is the warning line.** Investigate this month.
- **0% (billing at cost)** is the fire line. The conversation cannot wait another billing cycle.
- **Negative margin** means you are paying your client to be your client. You waited too long.

Parakeeto is stricter at project level: **55% delivery margin on P&L**, **70% on individual projects.** If you sit at 30–40%, you don't have a "lifestyle agency" — you have a slow leak.

---

## 1. The diagnostic — why is this client unprofitable?

Five causes. Each has a different conversation.

**1.1 Scope creep (most common).** Contract was for X. You're delivering X + Y + Z + "can you just quickly…" Parakeeto/BugHerd research puts the cost at **10–20% of project revenue on average.** *Tell:* if >20% of last-90-day hours map to work not in the SOW, this is it. Conversation is about scope, not price.

**1.2 Underestimated complexity.** You priced it wrong from the start. *Tell:* even in-scope hours blew the budget. Easiest repricing conversation — no one has to admit they're wrong.

**1.3 Legacy / under-market rate.** Signed three years ago. Costs went up; rate didn't. Sakas documents one agency averaging **$5/hour** vs a **$150/hour target** because a retainer was cut and nobody told the PM. ([Sakas — Client Dilution](https://sakasandcompany.com/client-dilution/)) *Tell:* effective rate is materially below what you'd quote today.

**1.4 Team mix wrong.** Running a Senior Strategist on Coordinator work. *Tell:* hours are reasonable; hourly cost of the people doing them is the outlier.

**1.5 Client mismanagement leaking in.** Their team won't make decisions, changes stakeholders, kicks revisions. *Tell:* your project lead spends >20% of time on coordination and rework. Process renegotiation, not price — though you may need both.

A client can have two or three at once. Identify the dominant one. That's what you lead with.

---

## 2. The 4 options

Pick before the meeting. Clients smell indecision.

**Option A — Reprice up.** Keep scope, raise rate. Use for legacy pricing (1.3) or underestimated complexity (1.2). Industry norm: **30–60 days' notice** on existing accounts. ([Wethos](https://www.wethos.co/blog/client-email-template-raising-rates))

**Option B — Narrow scope.** Keep rate, cut deliverables. Use for scope creep (1.1). Sakas's six-word phrase — *"Would you like an estimate for that?"* — exists to make the distinction visible in real time. ([Sakas — scope creep](https://sakasandcompany.com/stop-scope-creep/))

**Option C — Change the team mix.** Keep rate and scope, move work to less expensive delivery. Use for team mix (1.4). Client may not need to know — but manage the handoff if your senior person was the face of the account.

**Option D — Fire the client.** Exit with 30+ days' notice and referrals where possible. Use when math doesn't work even repriced to your new-business rate; client is bottom 10% by margin and top 10% by stress; you've had this conversation before and nothing changed; or behavior is unethical (no notice owed). Sakas's clearest case: a Virginia agency where **the three worst clients represented 9% of revenue, the worst less than 2%** — firing the bottom freed capacity that paid back in a quarter.

| Diagnosis | Default | Fallback |
|-----------|---------|----------|
| Scope creep | B (narrow) | A (reprice) |
| Underestimated complexity | A (reprice) | B (narrow) |
| Legacy rate | A (reprice) | D (transition) |
| Team mix wrong | C (change team) | A (reprice) |
| Client mismanagement | B + process | D (transition) |

## 3. Before the conversation: get the data right

Walk in with five numbers. Memorize them.

1. **Logged hours, last 90 days, by role.** "Senior strategist: 42h. Coordinator: 18h. Designer: 64h."
2. **Revenue from this client, last 90 days.** Net of discounts and unbilled WIP.
3. **Fully-loaded cost of delivery.** Hours × loaded cost per role (salary + benefits + overhead).
4. **Margin % = (Revenue − Cost) / Revenue.**
5. **What a comparable engagement would price at today** for a new client.

Useful sixth: **in-scope vs out-of-scope hours** — lets you say "78% of what we've done is what we sold; 22% is new" without sounding accusatory.

Bring one page, not a deck (decks read as defensive). Do not slide the page across until past the opening — numbers come after framing.

## 4. The opening script

The first two minutes decide whether this is a conversation or an ambush. Pick one based on relationship depth.

### Variant 1 — Value framing (newer / transactional)

> "Thanks for making time. I want to talk about how we keep this engagement working over the next year. When we scoped this last [March], the work looked like A, B, and C. Since then, the team has been delivering A, B, C, and increasingly D and E — which is great, your business is growing, and we're glad to be part of it. But the engagement we sold doesn't have D and E in it, and we're at a point where I either need to formally expand what we're doing together, or pull back to the original scope. I'd rather expand — and I want to walk you through what that looks like."

Never says "unprofitable." Says: *we're doing more than we sold — here are your two options.*

### Variant 2 — Transparency framing (mature, trust-based)

> "I want to be straight with you because I think we've earned that with each other. I ran the numbers on our engagement for the last quarter. We're delivering at about a [4]% margin, where our healthy floor is [20]%. The work is great, the relationship is great, but at this margin we can't keep our senior people on your account — and you don't want junior people running your account. So I want to talk through three options for how we fix this together, and you tell me which one feels right."

Names the number. Only works if you have enough relationship capital that "4%" lands as information, not a complaint.

### Variant 3 — Future framing (real growth story)

> "I've been thinking about where this engagement should be in twelve months. You've added [a second product line / two new markets / a new founder-led brand], and our current setup was built for where you were last year. I want to lay out what a 2026 version of working together looks like — what we'd cover, what it would cost, and what's not in the deal. The version we're running today won't carry you through next year, and I'd rather propose the next version than keep stretching this one."

Reprice without saying "your old rate doesn't work" — you're proposing a new engagement that happens to cost more.

## 5. Handling client pushback

You'll hear all four. Have a rehearsed response.

**"We'll churn / we'll have to leave."** The threat that makes founders fold. Don't. Negotiation research: **~60% of B2B buyers say sellers give discounts without being asked, or fold at the first pushback.** ([Boost Pricing](https://boostpricing.com/how-to-respond-to-price-pushback-ask-questions/))

> "I hear you, and I don't want that — you've been a great partner. But I'd rather have an honest conversation about whether this fits than have us limp along until the work suffers and you leave anyway. If the new structure doesn't work for you, let's talk about what would. What part feels like the dealbreaker?"

Then shut up. Next person to talk negotiates against themselves.

**"We can't afford it."** Often a posture. Offer trade-offs, not discounts.

> "That's fair, and I appreciate you saying it directly. Let's look at what we can take out so the number works for you. If we move [the monthly strategy session] to quarterly and pull [content QA] back to your team, we land at [$X]. Does that math work?"

Pricefx is direct: **never give without getting; structured concessions protect margin.** ([Pricefx](https://www.pricefx.com/learning-center/8-strategies-for-b2b-sales-teams-to-manage-price-increases))

**"This isn't a good time."** Translation: "I don't want to deal with this." It never will be.

> "I understand — and honestly, there's never going to be a quarter when this is easy. The reason I'm bringing it up now is the longer we wait, the bigger the gap gets. I'd rather do the small uncomfortable conversation today than the big one in six months. What if we set the new structure to start [60 days from now] — does that buy you enough runway?"

**"We already pay you a lot."** Sometimes they do — doesn't change the math.

> "You do, and we're grateful for it. The question isn't whether we're charging enough in absolute terms — it's whether we're charging enough for what we've ended up delivering. Let me show you the hour breakdown for the last 90 days, and you tell me if what you see matches what you thought you were paying for."

Now slide the one-pager across.

## 6. Closing the meeting with a commitment

Don't leave without a decision. Vague endings ("let me think about it") kill these conversations. Walk out with one of three things:

1. **A signed-or-verbally-agreed new structure** (A, B, or C). Confirm number, scope, start date.
2. **A scheduled second meeting within 7 days** with a named decision-maker. Calendar invite before you stand up.
3. **A mutually-acknowledged transition** (D). Confirm notice period, last day, referral offer.

The closing line:

> "Before we wrap — so I don't lose what we just talked about — what I'm hearing is [restate the option]. I'll send you a recap email by end of day tomorrow with the new terms and the start date. If anything in there doesn't match what we just agreed, you tell me and we fix it. Sound good?"

If they won't commit to anything, that's information. Diagnosis is probably 1.5 and the answer is probably D.

## 7. Follow-up email templates

Send within 24 hours. Half of margin conversations die because no one wrote down what was agreed. Sakas is explicit: **after the call, send a recap email reiterating what you discussed, with next steps.** ([Sakas — fire bad clients](https://sakasandcompany.com/how-to-fire-bad-clients/))

### Version A — Success (client agreed to the new structure)

> **Subject:** Recap — our updated engagement, starting [Date]
>
> [First name],
>
> Thanks again for the conversation. Putting what we agreed in writing so we're both holding the same picture.
>
> Starting **[Date]**:
>
> - **Scope:** [Bullet list of what's in — be specific.]
> - **Out of scope (handled separately or by your team):** [Bullet list of what's out.]
> - **Investment:** [$X] / month, billed on the [1st].
> - **Cadence:** [Weekly working session, monthly strategy review, quarterly business review.]
> - **Out-of-scope requests:** Quoted within two business days. No work begins until you approve.
>
> A new SOW lands by [Date] for signature. Current engagement continues unchanged until [Date].
>
> Thanks for working through this with me. The new structure lets us put our best people on your account and protect the quality you signed up for. Reply if anything doesn't match what we discussed.
>
> — [Your name]

### Version B — Pushback / no decision yet (holding the line)

> **Subject:** Following up — and a date to circle back
>
> [First name],
>
> Thanks for the candid conversation. To make sure I represented things fairly, here's what I shared:
>
> - Over the last 90 days, our team logged **[X] hours** on your account, against a scope built for roughly **[Y]**.
> - At current pricing, effective margin is **[Z]%**, against our internal floor of **20%**.
> - Three paths on the table: [A] reprice to **[$X]/mo**, [B] narrow scope back to the original SOW at current price, or [C] transition to a different team mix.
>
> I know you wanted time to think — fair. I've put a 30-minute hold on our calendars for **[Date / time]** to land on a direction. Send three options if a different time works better.
>
> To be transparent: the current structure isn't sustainable past **[Date]**, so I do need a decision by then. I'd much rather find a version of this that works for both of us than end a relationship I've enjoyed building.
>
> Talk soon,
> [Your name]

## 8. What NOT to say

- **"We're losing money on you."** Positions the client as the problem and you as the victim. Replace with: *"The engagement has drifted from what we scoped."*
- **"You're a difficult client."** Tells them they're the villain. If they truly are, fire them — don't lecture them.
- **"I'm sorry to bring this up…" / "I hate to do this…"** Pre-emptive apologies signal you don't believe your own ask. Negotiations Ninja: **when price pressure builds, buyers test your composure. If you look rattled, they press harder.** ([Negotiations Ninja](https://negotiations.ninja/blog/overcoming-price-pushbacks-negotiation-techniques-for-premium-b2b-offers/))
- **"Our costs have gone up."** Nobody cares about your costs. Reframe around the work and the value.
- **"What would it take to keep you?"** Turns the conversation into a discount auction.
- **"Let me check with my partner / CFO."** Bring decision authority into the room or don't have the meeting.
- **"This is just how the industry is moving."** Vague macro-framing reads as a dodge. Be specific to *this* engagement.

---

## 9. Common mistakes

**Too late.** Founders wait three, six, nine months hoping the next project absorbs the loss. It doesn't. By month nine you sound desperate, which means you fold. *Rule:* first red month is a watch, second is a meeting, third is a management failure.

**Wrong person in the room.** Meeting with the day-to-day contact who has no authority to renegotiate. *Rule:* margin conversations happen with the SOW signer or their direct boss. Escalate if you have to.

**No concrete ask.** You walk in to "talk about the engagement" and walk out having vented. *Rule:* decide which option (A/B/C/D) you're pushing for *before* booking the invite. Write the new number down. Practice saying it out loud without flinching.

**No follow-through.** Meeting goes well. Three weeks later two out-of-scope requests land in Slack and nobody quoted them. *Rule:* day one of the new structure, every out-of-scope request gets Sakas's six-word reply — *"Would you like an estimate for that?"* — for 60 days, no exceptions. The habit holds itself after that.

**Treating it as one-time.** Margin alerts are recurring discipline, not an annual exception. Healthiest agencies look at per-client margin **monthly** and run a low-grade version of this conversation **quarterly** with every account — as a check-in, not a crisis.

---

## TL;DR

- A red margin card is a meeting to schedule, this week — not a problem to study.
- Diagnose the dominant cause. The diagnosis picks the option.
- One page of numbers. One decision you're pushing for.
- Open with value, transparency, or future framing — never apology.
- Hold the line on pushback. Trade concessions for concessions; never discount on demand.
- Leave with a decision, a date, or a transition. Not "we'll see."
- Recap in writing within 24 hours.
- The conversation you don't want to have today is the one you'll have anyway in three months — with less leverage, less trust, and worse numbers.
