# The difficult client diagnostic — fix or fire?

Almost every agency owner is carrying at least one client they should have ended a quarter ago. The reasons are predictable: the revenue is real, the replacement is hypothetical, the conversation feels harder than the pain. So the relationship limps along, the team quietly burns down, and the margin you thought you were protecting evaporates into unbillable hours and a senior delivery lead's resignation letter.

This playbook is the diagnostic for that decision. It gives you a scoring framework, an honest cost comparison, and verbatim scripts for the two conversations you actually have to have: the reset (fix) and the off-ramp (fire). It is built on advice from agency consultants who do this for a living — Karl Sakas, Jason Swenk, Drew McLellan — combined with what the data says about scope creep, profitability, and team burnout in service businesses.

If you only remember one number from this document, remember this one: industry studies suggest 20–30% of agency clients are unprofitable or break-even, and agencies that prune the bottom 10–15% often see net margins lift 5–10 percentage points while reducing team stress ([101 Agencies](https://101agencies.com/insights/blog/agency-profit-margins-benchmarks-improvement)). The cost of keeping a bad client is rarely the math you did when you signed them.

## 1. The 5 categories of difficult clients

Different problems demand different responses. Misdiagnosing the category is how owners waste months trying to fix a behavior problem with a process fix, or vice versa. Identify which category (or combination) you are dealing with before you decide anything.

### 1.1 The Late Payer

**Pattern:** Net-30 invoices land in your bank at day 45, 60, sometimes 75. There is always a story — AP cycle changes, a missed approval, the bookkeeper on leave. Individually each excuse is plausible. As a pattern, you are functioning as their interest-free line of credit.

**Tell:** Pull last 12 invoices. If average days-to-pay exceeds your stated terms by more than 15 days, or more than 30% of invoices land late, this is a Late Payer — not a one-off.

**Diagnostic question:** Are they late because they cannot pay (cash flow), because their AP process is broken (operations), or because they have learned you tolerate it (culture)?

The first is fixable with a payment plan. The second is fixable with a payment-portal change and a finance-to-finance escalation. The third only ends when you make late payment more painful for them than for you.

### 1.2 The Scope Creeper

**Pattern:** "Quick question," "tiny tweak," "while you're in there." Each request is small enough to feel petty to refuse. In aggregate, the team logs 30%+ over budget every month and nobody can explain where the hours went.

**Tell:** Compare scoped hours to actual hours across the last 3 months. PMI 2024 data puts scope creep at around 37% of projects on average, with bottom-performers above 40% ([101 Agencies](https://101agencies.com/insights/blog/agency-profit-margins-benchmarks-improvement)). A retainer running 20%+ over budgeted hours for three months in a row is a scope problem, not a one-off heavy month.

**Diagnostic question:** Has the client ever signed a change order, or do you not have a change-order process at all? If the latter, the problem is partly yours — fix the process before you fire.

### 1.3 The Team-Disrespecter

**Pattern:** Condescending language on calls. Public criticism of junior staff in front of the client's own team. Late-night Slack messages with no urgency. Treating account managers as a help desk to be shouted at.

**Tell:** Ask three direct questions in your next 1:1s — "How are calls with [client] going? Anything I should know about how they communicate? Would you be sad if we stopped working with them?" If two of three team members hesitate or describe specific incidents, you have a behavior problem.

This is the category where 57% of web professionals who have fired a client did so — unrealistic expectations or outright toxicity ([WhatConverts](https://www.whatconverts.com/blog/dealing-with-difficult-clients/)). It is also the category where ignoring it costs you your best people.

### 1.4 The Mismatched-Expectations Client

**Pattern:** They expected SEO results in 6 weeks. They thought the retainer included strategy consulting. They believed "social media" meant TikTok ads. The work isn't bad. The expectations were never aligned in the first place.

**Tell:** Calls and emails consistently include phrases like "I thought…" "Aren't you supposed to…" "Last agency did…" There is no specific behavior problem — just a chronic gap between what they expected and what they bought.

Jason Swenk's framing is useful here: "there is no bad agency client — only a bad prospect or a bad process" ([Jason Swenk](https://jasonswenk.com/no-bad-agency-client/)). Mismatch is often a qualification failure at the sale, which means it may be fixable with a scope reset — but only if both sides genuinely want the same outcome.

### 1.5 The Strategically Unprofitable Client

**Pattern:** They pay on time. They are pleasant. The team likes them. But when you do the math — billable hours plus account management, status calls, slack, scope creep, internal coordination — the net margin is at or below zero. Drew McLellan's threshold: if a client is below 10% account profitability and you don't have a plan to grow them, that is a red flag ([Agency Outsight on McLellan](https://www.agencyoutsight.com/podcast/episode-135)).

**Tell:** Compute fully-loaded margin per client for the trailing 90 days. Include AM time, weekly status calls, internal review meetings, scope-creep hours, and any rework. If margin is below your agency-wide net target and there's no realistic upgrade path within 90 days, this is strategic unprofitability.

This category is the most insidious — there's no behavior to point at. The relationship is "fine." It is also the easiest to fix because there is no emotional charge: you can grow the engagement, restructure pricing, or part ways without any drama.

## 2. The diagnostic scorecard

Score the client 1–5 on each of four axes. Total it. The total tells you what to do.

| Axis | What you are measuring | 1 (worst) | 5 (best) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Margin %** | Fully-loaded net margin: revenue minus all delivery hours, AM time, scope creep, rework | <0% (losing money) | >30% (above agency target) |
| **Hours overhead** | Non-billable comms, internal meetings, slack threads, escalations per week | >10 hrs/week of non-billable overhead per client | <2 hrs/week non-billable |
| **Team morale impact** | How the team responds to seeing this client's name on the calendar | Specific staff have asked off the account or threatened to quit | Team enjoys the work; cited as a favorite |
| **Strategic value** | Reference logo, case study potential, referral source, expansion runway, learning opportunity | None — pure revenue, no other value | High — logo opens doors, gives referrals, expanding scope |

**Scoring buckets:**

- **17–20: Grow.** This is a top-quartile client. Invest in deepening the relationship, set up a QBR, identify expansion.
- **13–16: Keep & monitor.** Healthy. Look for the one axis dragging the score and improve it.
- **9–12: Fix.** There is real value here but a real problem. Run the reset conversation (Section 5). Set a 60–90 day window. If no measurable improvement, move to fire.
- **5–8: Fire.** You are subsidizing this client with your team's health and your other clients' margin. Plan the off-ramp.
- **4: Fire now.** Whatever revenue you are losing is less than the damage you are absorbing.

This is a sharpened version of Karl Sakas's Client Ranking Matrix (current value × future potential), which is the framework most agency consultants point to ([Sakas & Company](https://sakasandcompany.com/how-to-fire-bad-clients/)). The four-axis variant pulls in the team morale and overhead dimensions that pure value-matrix scoring misses.

**Cadence:** Score every active client once a quarter. Anyone in the 5–12 band gets discussed in the next leadership meeting. Anyone in the 4 band gets an off-ramp plan within two weeks.

## 3. The cost of staying

The mistake owners make is comparing the client's MRR against zero. The real comparison is MRR against the **fully loaded cost of staying**. That includes:

**Team burnout and churn risk.** Call center research found total turnover cost can reach ~$20,000 per agent, and unresolved burnout in a 100-person team can compound to over $1M in costs through productivity loss and quality degradation ([Clearsource](https://clearsourcebpo.com/how-call-center-agent-burnout-kills-performance/)). Agencies are smaller but the math is worse — losing a senior strategist or lead developer means months of recruiting plus institutional knowledge walking out. A toxic client who triggers one senior departure has cost you more than a year of their MRR.

**Opportunity cost.** Every hour your team spends managing escalations, soothing the difficult client, or absorbing scope creep is an hour not spent on (a) better clients, (b) new business, or (c) building IP. If a difficult client consumes 10 hours of unbilled overhead per week and your blended billable rate is $150/hr, that's $78,000/year in opportunity cost — before you count the morale tax.

**Hidden margin leakage.** Scope creep alone eats 15–30% of potential profit at most agencies ([101 Agencies](https://101agencies.com/insights/blog/agency-profit-margins-benchmarks-improvement)). A retainer that looks like 25% margin on paper often runs at 5–10% once you load in the actual hours.

**Brand and team-recruiting damage.** If your difficult client is public-facing in their industry, the way you tolerate their behavior becomes part of how senior candidates evaluate you. "I left my last agency because they wouldn't fire [X]" is something candidates say in interviews. It is also what departing employees say in Glassdoor reviews.

**The message you send to the team.** Karl Sakas's framing is direct: "When you continue to ignore clients behaving badly, you're sending a clear message to your team — that the revenue from one client matters more than your employees' happiness" ([Sakas & Company](https://sakasandcompany.com/how-to-fire-bad-clients/)). That message compounds. Once it's internalized, it doesn't matter what your culture deck says.

**Sum it up honestly.** For a difficult client at $10K/month MRR:

- Annual revenue: $120K
- Less unbilled overhead (10 hrs/wk × $150 × 50 weeks): -$75K
- Less scope creep (20% of delivery hours unbilled): -$15K
- Less proportional contribution to senior turnover risk (assume 10% of one $30K replacement cost): -$3K
- **Net contribution: ~$27K, or about 22% of headline revenue.**

That's the real comparison. Not $120K vs zero. $27K vs the new client you can land in the 6 months of mental and team bandwidth you free up.

## 4. The cost of firing

Be honest in both directions. Firing has real costs and you should price them before pulling the trigger.

**Revenue gap.** You lose MRR on the termination date. If they are >10% of revenue, this is a serious cash event — and a signal you violated Sakas's concentration rule ("no single client more than 10% of your revenues" — [Sakas & Company](https://sakasandcompany.com/time-to-fire-a-client/)). Model your runway with the client gone. If runway falls below 6 months, sequence the firing alongside pipeline build rather than firing first and panicking second.

**Transition work.** Plan to spend 20–60 hours per fired client on handoff: documentation, asset transfer, knowledge transfer to their next provider, any agreed-upon overlap. Bill for it if your contract allows, but plan for it either way.

**Reputation risk.** Most fired clients do not publicly badmouth professionally-fired agencies. The risk is concentrated in the disrespectful/abusive category — the same people who treat your team badly will also write the angry LinkedIn post. Mitigate by (a) firing professionally, (b) giving notice, (c) helping them transition, (d) never matching their tone in writing.

**Refund / credit liability.** Check your contract. Pre-paid retainers, deposits, and unearned-revenue lines are owed back. Surprises here turn an amicable off-ramp into a small-claims situation.

**Team morale tax (yes, even the right direction).** Some team members — especially the ones who took the client on originally, or the salesperson who closed them — will feel personally about the firing. ZenPilot's specific advice: "include the sales rep who sold the client" in the firing process ([ZenPilot](https://www.zenpilot.com/blog/why-your-agency-sales-reps-need-to-be-involved-in-firing-bad-clients)). Don't blindside your team with a fait accompli.

**Honest calculation:**

- Lost revenue (6 months until backfilled, 50% probability of full backfill): factor MRR × 6 × 0.5
- Transition hours × your blended rate
- Any refund/credit obligation
- Your time on the conversation, the email, and the wind-down: budget 8–10 hours of owner time

If the diagnostic scorecard says fire and the cost of firing is less than the annualized cost of staying, the math has already made the decision for you.

## 5. The "fix" conversation

If the client scored 9–12, you owe them — and yourself — one honest reset conversation before you fire. Three things determine whether it works:

1. **You, not a junior, run the meeting.** The owner or the senior partner. Sending a junior account manager to deliver bad news to a difficult client is one of the surest ways to lose the junior and not fix the relationship.
2. **Video or in-person, never email.** Tone and body language carry the message.
3. **You go in with a written follow-up ready.** Whatever they agree to in the call, you recap by email within 4 hours. No agreement, no follow-up = no fix.

Frame the conversation around shared outcomes, not your frustration. The opening line in all three variants below is some version of: "I want to talk about how we make this work, because right now it isn't, and I think you can feel that too."

### Variant A — Payment problem (Late Payer)

> "I wanted to set up some time because I need to talk about how invoicing has been running. Over the last six months, our invoices to [Client] have averaged 47 days to pay against Net-30 terms. I'm raising this directly because it's at a point where it's affecting how we can staff your account, and that affects the work we do for you.
>
> Two questions for you. First — is there something going on internally with your AP process that we should know about, or is there a way we can change our invoicing to make it easier on your side? Second — going forward, we'd like to move to either auto-pay on a credit card or ACH on day-of-invoice. That solves it on both sides. Would that work for you?
>
> I want to be straight with you: if we can't get to a workable solution on payment terms in the next 30 days, we won't be able to continue the engagement, and I'd rather have this conversation now than be surprised later."

Sakas's framing is to use passive voice when escalating with chronic late payers ("Do you know when [Company] plans to pay?" rather than "When are you going to pay?") to lower defensiveness ([Sakas & Company](https://sakasandcompany.com/clients-who-keep-paying-late/)). For the reset call you can be more direct — but follow up the call with a written summary in that softer register.

### Variant B — Scope problem (Scope Creeper)

> "I wanted to talk about how we're tracking against the retainer. For the last three months we've delivered 30%, 28%, and 41% over the budgeted hours. That tells me the scope of what you actually need is bigger than what we agreed when we signed.
>
> I see two paths and I want your input on which fits better. One — we keep the retainer at its current dollar value and we work together to ruthlessly prioritize what fits inside it, and anything else goes through a change-order process I'll walk you through. Two — we acknowledge the real scope and resize the retainer to match it, which means a bigger monthly number but no surprises on either side.
>
> What I can't do is keep absorbing the overage, because the math doesn't work and eventually the quality slips. Which of those two feels right for where [Client] actually is?"

Jennifer Bourn's framing on the change-order conversation is useful: present options, don't corner the client; reset internal behavior (who can approve extras, how requests are logged) the same week you reset external behavior, or the external reset won't stick ([Jennifer Bourn](https://jenniferbourn.com/script-address-prevent-scope-creep/)).

### Variant C — Behavior problem (Team-Disrespecter)

This is the hardest of the three. It is also the one where, more than half the time, the reset doesn't take and you end up firing anyway. Run it anyway — for the legal/professional record, and because sometimes people genuinely don't know how they come across.

> "I wanted to raise something directly with you, owner to [their role]. Over the last [period], a couple of incidents have come up in how the team experienced some of our calls and messages — specifically [name 1–2 concrete examples without exaggerating]. I'm not raising this to relitigate them. I'm raising it because how my team is treated is something I have to take seriously, and I won't be able to keep them on accounts where the relationship feels disrespectful.
>
> What I'd like to agree on is how we communicate going forward. [Specific ask: e.g., 'escalations come through me, not directly at the account manager on Slack at 11 pm,' or 'feedback gets shared in our weekly call, not in front of your internal team.']
>
> I value the work we do together and I'd like to keep doing it. I also want to be honest that if the pattern continues after today, we'll need to end the engagement, and I'd rather you hear that from me clearly now."

Sakas's softer version of this conversation — when the offending behavior is from one individual at the client and there's a supervisor available — is to request that specific person be removed from interactions with your team rather than firing the whole account ([Sakas & Company](https://sakasandcompany.com/how-to-fire-bad-clients/)). That's the ultimatum alternative. Use it when the wider account is healthy and one person is the problem.

**Always follow up in writing within 4 hours of the call.** Two paragraphs. What we discussed. What we agreed. What changes by when. This document is what you will refer to in 30 days when you decide whether the reset worked.

## 6. The "fire" conversation

If the scorecard says fire, or the reset failed, run the off-ramp. Six rules.

**Rule 1: Call, don't email — but follow with email.** Sakas, ZenPilot, and every agency consultant on this topic agree: pick up the phone or do a video call. Email-only is what hostile firings look like, and the client will tell that story. The written email follows the call, not replaces it. ([Sakas & Company](https://sakasandcompany.com/how-to-fire-bad-clients/))

**Rule 2: The owner makes the call.** Not the account manager. Not the salesperson alone. If you sold the client, you fire the client — and bring the AM in for the handoff. Junior staff should not be the messenger.

**Rule 3: Give at least 30 days' notice for paid work.** Standard across every source on this topic. For long-term retainers or complex builds, 30–60 days is more appropriate ([SchedulingKit](https://schedulingkit.com/blog/how-to-fire-client-professionally), [Sakas & Company](https://sakasandcompany.com/how-to-fire-bad-clients/)). Check your contract for shorter or longer notice clauses before you say a number.

**Rule 4: Don't lie about the reason — but don't overshare.** "We're restructuring our service mix and your account is no longer a fit" is true for almost every firing if you stretch the definition slightly. You do not owe the client a full diagnostic of why they were a bad partner. You do owe them honesty about what is happening.

**Rule 5: Don't reverse course.** If they come back with a higher fee, an apology, a promise to be different — the answer is no. Wavering after firing trains the client to push, and undermines the team's trust that you meant it.

**Rule 6: Help the transition.** Offer 2–3 names of agencies they might talk to. Help them onboard their next provider. This protects your reputation more than any other single thing.

### The call script

> "Thanks for jumping on. I want to talk to you directly about the engagement going forward. After looking at how the last [period] has gone, I've made the decision that [Agency] won't be the right partner for [Client] beyond [date — 30+ days out]. I wanted to tell you that on the phone rather than in an email.
>
> Here's what that looks like. Between now and [date], we'll complete [specific deliverables]. We'll prepare a full handoff package — access transfers, working files, account credentials, anything you need to move smoothly to whoever you choose next. If it helps, I can share a short list of 2–3 firms I think are a stronger fit for where [Client] is going.
>
> I'll send a written summary of everything I just said within the hour. And I want to be straightforward — I'm not looking to renegotiate, I've made the call, and I want to make the off-ramp as smooth as I can for you."

Pause. Let them respond. They may push back, ask why, get defensive, or be relieved. None of these change the outcome — but listening to whichever response you get is how the conversation ends with dignity intact.

### The email template (sent within 1 hour of the call)

> Subject: Confirming our conversation — engagement wrap-up
>
> Hi [Name],
>
> Thanks for the call this morning. As discussed, I'm writing to confirm that [Agency] will be wrapping up our engagement with [Client] as of [Date — at least 30 days out]. This email is to put what we agreed in writing.
>
> Between now and [Date], we'll:
> - Complete [specific deliverable 1]
> - Complete [specific deliverable 2]
> - Prepare a handoff package containing [files, credentials, documentation]
> - Be available for [X hours] of transition support with whichever provider you choose next
>
> The final invoice for [period] will be sent on [date] under standard Net-[N] terms. [If applicable: We will refund the unearned portion of your [month] retainer of $X within 14 days of [Date].]
>
> If it's useful, I'm happy to share a short list of agencies I think would be a stronger fit for where [Client] is going. Just say the word.
>
> Thank you for the opportunity to work with you, and I wish [Client] the best.
>
> [Name]
> [Title]

Adapted from working templates collected by Millo ([Millo](https://millo.co/when-to-fire-a-design-client)) and SchedulingKit ([SchedulingKit](https://schedulingkit.com/blog/how-to-fire-client-professionally)). Keep it factual, dated, and unemotional. This email is also your legal record.

## 7. The transition plan

Notice given. Email sent. Now you have 30–60 days to wind down without damaging your team, your reputation, or your final invoice. Run this as a project with a named owner.

**Week 1 of notice period:**
- Internal kickoff: who on your team works on what during wind-down, and what stops immediately (e.g., new strategy work) vs. what continues (existing in-flight deliverables).
- Inventory all credentials, accounts, and access — both ways. Theirs in your systems, yours in theirs.
- Confirm any pre-paid amounts, deposits, or unearned revenue. Calculate the refund (if any) and put it in the calendar for processing on termination date.

**Weeks 2–3:**
- Complete committed deliverables. Do not start anything new.
- Build the handoff package: brand guidelines, working files, ad accounts, analytics access, passwords, vendor contacts, historical reports, anything they paid for that they own.
- If the client requests a recommendation, share 2–3 vetted alternatives. Do not steer them toward a competitor you dislike — that is what is now in the news cycle.

**Week 4 / final week:**
- Final invoice goes out on the date agreed in the email.
- Asset transfer completes. Confirm receipt in writing.
- Remove your team's access to client systems. Have them remove yours.
- Send a polite closing email: "All assets transferred as agreed. Best of luck with the next chapter."

**NDA / IP cleanup:** Your team retains its obligations under the NDA past termination. Remind them in writing — one Slack message in the team channel: "Reminder that the [Client] NDA continues to apply after [date]. Don't repost any of their internal materials, ad accounts, or strategy decks in portfolios without written permission." This protects you legally and culturally signals that you take confidentiality seriously even with clients you fired.

**Refund handling:** If you owe money back, pay it on or before the termination date — never later. The single most reputation-damaging move in this entire process is dragging out a refund to a fired client. They will tell that story for a decade.

## 8. The aftermath

The firing is the start of a 90-day internal process, not the end of an event.

**Communicating with the team.** Tell the team the day after the call to the client (not before — leaks happen). Three points: (1) we ended the [Client] engagement; (2) here is the honest reason at a high level, framed around fit and standards, not anger; (3) here is what happens to the people on that account. The third point matters most — your team is hearing "is my job at risk?" before they hear anything else. Answer that directly. Layoffs because of the firing are sometimes unavoidable; pretending they aren't, then doing them two weeks later, is the worst path.

**Communicating to other clients.** Default: don't. Other clients do not need to know you fired someone. If asked by a client who shares the firee's industry or knows them personally, the answer is "we mutually wrapped up the engagement when it stopped being a fit." That's it. No details, no shade.

**Internal post-mortem.** Within two weeks, run a 60-minute leadership debrief. What signals did we miss in the sales process? What category of client was this? Did our qualification process screen for it, and if not, what's the change? Jason Swenk's framing is the right lens: prevention is cheaper than firing ([Jason Swenk](https://jasonswenk.com/no-bad-agency-client/)). Every firing should produce one concrete change to your intake process — a new question on the qualification call, a new clause in the contract, a new red-flag heuristic the AE checks.

**The freed-up capacity.** This is the part owners under-plan. The team that was on the difficult account now has 30–40% of their week back. Don't immediately fill it with a new client — that resets the burnout clock. For the first 30 days post-departure, deliberately use that time on: catching up on technical debt, internal IP development, sales support for new pipeline, a senior team member's stalled certification. Two reasons. First, you signal to the team that ending the bad client meant relief, not a treadmill swap. Second, the IP/sales-support work compounds into next quarter's better margin. Then start filling the slot.

## 9. Common mistakes

These are the seven failure modes that turn a clean firing into a story the agency will be telling forever.

**1. Firing in anger via email.** The most common, the most damaging. Anger is data — it tells you a decision is overdue. It is not the energy you make the decision in. If you are angry, schedule the call for 48 hours later. The email arrives in the right tone once you've slept.

**2. No transition period.** Walking out same-day is what abusive parties do, and that becomes how the client describes you. 30 days minimum on paid work. Always.

**3. Ghosting.** Letting the relationship die slowly by being unresponsive, missing meetings, stretching delivery — instead of having the actual conversation. This is the most cowardly version of firing and the team can see you doing it. Have the call.

**4. Lying about the reason.** "We're restructuring our service offering and your account is no longer a fit" can be true. "We have a family emergency" is a lie that surfaces 3 months later when the client sees you onboarding two new clients. Stick to honest, neutral framing.

**5. Delegating the call to a junior.** ZenPilot's framing: bring the salesperson in, but the owner runs the meeting ([ZenPilot](https://www.zenpilot.com/blog/why-your-agency-sales-reps-need-to-be-involved-in-firing-bad-clients)). Sending a 24-year-old account coordinator to fire a difficult senior buyer is how you lose the coordinator, not how you end the engagement.

**6. Reversing course when they offer more money.** The math is identical the day after the call as the day before. If you would not have signed them today at 1.5x the fee, the answer is still no. Stand the decision.

**7. Skipping the post-mortem.** Most agency owners fire a client, feel relief, and never ask why the client got past intake. The next one is already in your pipeline. The single highest-leverage hour you spend on this whole topic is the 60 minutes you spend retro'ing your qualification process.

## 10. Cost of NOT firing — anonymized example

A composite case, drawn from agency consultant casework (Karl Sakas's published Q&As, Scoro's profitability case study, and the patterns documented across the sources cited in this playbook). Numbers and details are illustrative but the shape is realistic for a 20-person agency.

**The setup.** A 20-person digital agency held a $14K/month retainer with a B2B SaaS client — about 8% of MRR. The client was a Mismatched-Expectations + Team-Disrespecter combo. Friendly to the owner, sharp with the team, particularly with the senior PM. Scope had drifted 25% over the budgeted hours for nine consecutive months. Margin on paper was 22%; fully loaded, it was below 5%.

**The decision delay.** The owner ran the diagnostic twice — once at month 6, once at month 9. Both times the scorecard landed at 9 (borderline fix-or-fire). Both times the owner chose "fix" — but didn't actually run a reset conversation. Just hoped it would settle.

**The cost of staying, measured at month 12:**

- Senior PM resigned in month 11, citing the account specifically in the exit interview. Replacement search: 4 months. Onboarding loss: $40K in lost productivity plus $15K in recruiting. **Direct cost: $55K.**
- Two other team members had quietly asked to be rotated off the account. The owner had absorbed those hours himself, working ~6 hours/week on the account directly. At his blended owner-rate equivalent of $250/hr: **~$78K of opportunity cost over the year.**
- Scope creep absorbed (~25% × $14K × 12 months × 60% cost): **~$25K in pure margin leakage.**
- Two prospective clients in adjacent verticals were declined because the team was "full." Estimated annual value of one new retainer: $100K+. **Opportunity cost: at least $50K of expected value in declined pipeline.**

**Total cost of staying for 12 months: ~$208K of value destruction against $168K of headline revenue.**

The client was eventually fired in month 13 — after the PM's replacement also asked off the account. The firing took 50 minutes on a call and one email. Within 90 days post-firing, the team's NPS rose, two stalled internal initiatives shipped, and one of the two previously-declined prospects was signed.

**The lesson.** The cost of firing was a one-time $14K MRR gap, partially backfilled within 60 days. The cost of *not* firing — borne every single month for nine months past the moment it became obvious — was multiples of that. The math was never close. The owner just couldn't see it from inside the relationship.

If you have a client in the 9–12 scorecard band right now, and you cannot point to a specific reset conversation you have run in the last 30 days, that client is the one this playbook is for. Run the reset. If it doesn't take in 60 days, run the off-ramp. The team you have, and the team you want to have, are watching.
