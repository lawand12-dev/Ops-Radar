# Cash flow recovery — three levers when cash is tight

You opened the bank account, saw payroll on Friday, and felt your stomach drop. That moment is what this playbook is for.

You have more levers than panic suggests, but you have to pull them in order. This covers the three levers — accelerate inbound, slow outbound, raise capital — plus diagnosis, team and vendor conversations, and when to bring in professional help.

---

## 1. Diagnose the crunch type before pulling any lever

The biggest mistake is treating every crunch the same. Pulling the wrong lever — a line of credit when you actually have a sales gap — buys 60 days and a bigger hole. Four types:

**Slow-pay (A/R problem).** Profitable on paper, but DSO crept from 35 to 65 days. Most common, most fixable — money exists, just not in your account. Diagnostic: overdue A/R (30+ days) exceeds next two pay periods. The [2025 Professional Services DSO benchmark is 30–45 days for top performers and 50–60 for average firms](https://www.growfin.ai/resources/dso-benchmarks-and-calculator); above 60 days signals systemic issues.

**High-overhead (cost problem).** Revenue steady, A/R healthy, but operating costs grew faster than gross margin. Classic version: kept three contractors past project end. Diagnostic: gross margin under 50%, or payroll-as-percent-of-revenue above 60%.

**Revenue-gap (sales problem).** Pipeline dried up. Two big clients churned without replacement. Most dangerous — slowest to fix. Diagnostic: booked revenue for next 90 days below operating cost baseline.

**Growth-burn (intentional, mismanaged).** Hired ahead of revenue and misjudged the gap. Fix is timeline management, not panic cuts. Diagnostic: you can articulate bookings that will close the gap, and they're real (signed LOIs).

**Action:** 30 minutes today on a one-page diagnosis — which type, dollars, weeks. Without this, everything below is a guess.

## 2. Lever 1: Accelerate inbound

Cheapest lever — money that's already yours.

### Payment reminder cadence

Move from "we'll email when we get around to it" to a mechanical cadence. [Karl Sakas's coaching clients who dedicate time each week to A/R recover 20% of overdue balances in the first two weeks](https://sakasandcompany.com/accounts-receivable-escalation/).

| Day | Action | Tone |
|-----|--------|------|
| Day 0 (invoice send) | Invoice + confirmation request | Friendly |
| Day -3 (before due) | Gentle reminder due date approaching | Friendly |
| Day +7 (past due) | First overdue email | Professional |
| Day +14 | Second overdue, mention late fee | Firm |
| Day +30 | Third overdue, escalation warning | Serious |
| Day +45 | Stop-work notice | Formal |
| Day +60 | Demand letter or collections handoff | Legal |

[Industry guidance: 3–5 reminders over 30–45 days with progressively increasing firmness](https://paymenthunter.bot/blog/overdue-invoice-email-template). The biggest determinant of recovery is whether you actually send them on time.

### Early-pay discounts: 2/10 net 30 math

"2% off if paid within 10 days, full amount due in 30." On $10,000: pay Day 10 → $9,800; Day 30 → $10,000.

[Annualized cost of 2/10 net 30 is ~36.7% — clients skipping your discount are effectively borrowing at 36.7% APR. Early-payment discounts accelerate payment by 10–15 days on average](https://www.highradius.com/resources/Blog/2-10-net-30/).

**Offer tactically** — to your largest A/R balances during a crunch, not as a permanent price cut.

### When to send to collections

60+ days overdue with no payment plan after direct outreach. Beyond 90 days, recovery rate drops below 50%. Agencies wait too long out of guilt — by handoff, it's uncollectible. Collections agencies charge 25–50% contingency. Half of something beats all of nothing.

### Switch new clients to upfront billing

Every new client during or after the crunch, no exceptions:

- **50% upfront, 50% on delivery** (project work)
- **Month-in-advance retainer** (not arrears)
- **ACH autopay** required for retainers over $5,000/month

[Top performers keep DSO at 30–45 days by structuring billing this way](https://www.growfin.ai/resources/dso-benchmarks-and-calculator), not by chasing harder.

## 3. Collection email scripts

Use verbatim. Edit bracketed fields. Don't soften.

### 7 days overdue

> **Subject:** Invoice #1042 — quick reminder
>
> Hi [Name],
>
> Just a quick note that invoice #1042 for $[amount], due [date], shows as outstanding on our end. Possible it crossed wires with AP, so wanted to flag.
>
> Could you confirm when we should expect payment? If there's a hold-up or something to clarify, let me know. Invoice reattached.
>
> Thanks, [Your name]

### 30 days overdue

> **Subject:** Invoice #1042 — now 30 days past due
>
> Hi [Name],
>
> Invoice #1042 for $[amount], originally due [date], is now 30 days past due. We haven't received payment or a response to previous reminders.
>
> Per our service agreement, accounts over 30 days overdue are subject to a 1.5% monthly late fee, which we've added. Updated invoice attached: $[new amount].
>
> Please process payment by [date — 7 days out] or reply with a payment plan. If we don't hear back, we'll need to pause work on [project/retainer] until the account is current.
>
> Happy to jump on a call today or tomorrow.
>
> [Your name]

### 60 days overdue

> **Subject:** Final notice before collections — Invoice #1042
>
> [Name],
>
> Invoice #1042 is now 60 days past due. Including late fees, outstanding balance is $[new amount]. We've sent reminders on [dates], and work has been paused since [date].
>
> This is our final attempt to resolve directly. We need one of the following by [date — 7 days out]:
>
> 1. Full payment of $[amount] by wire or ACH, OR
> 2. A signed payment plan with first payment received by that date
>
> If we don't receive a response by [date], this account will be transferred to [collections agency] for recovery, and we'll reserve our rights under section [X] of the MSA.
>
> We'd much prefer to resolve this with you. Please call me at [phone] today.
>
> [Your name], [Title]

### Formal demand (90+ days)

> **Subject:** Formal demand for payment — Invoice #1042
>
> [Legal name of debtor],
>
> This letter constitutes formal demand for payment of $[total] owed to [Your Company Legal Name] under invoice #1042 dated [original date], pursuant to the MSA executed [contract date].
>
> Despite prior notices on [dates], the balance remains unpaid. Unless payment in full is received at [bank details] within 10 business days — by [specific date] — [Your Company] will pursue all available remedies, including collections, filing in [appropriate court], and recovery of legal fees per section [X] of the MSA.
>
> [Your name], [Title], [Your Company]

Send by both email AND certified mail with return receipt. The paper trail matters if it ends up in court.

## 4. Lever 2: Slow outbound

### Vendor renegotiation

[U.S. Chamber of Commerce: don't wait until you're 30 days late](https://www.uschamber.com/co/run/business-financing/how-to-negotiate-payment-terms-with-vendors). Reach out 30 days *before* the bill is due, while you still have credibility.

Categorize: **critical** (hosting, core SaaS, payroll) get transparency and a specific ask; **important but replaceable** (marketing tools, secondary SaaS) downgrade or cancel; **non-essential** (anything you'd be embarrassed to admit you pay for) cancel today.

For critical vendors, in order of preference: (1) extended terms — net 15 to net 60 for three months; (2) payment plan on balance — $24,000 split into 6 months at $4,000; (3) skip + extension — skip June, resume July, add skipped amount to back end. Never just stop paying without a conversation.

### Contractor payment terms

Standard agency mistake: paying contractors net 15 while collecting net 45 — financing clients with contractor money. New agreements: net 30, paid when client pays. Existing: have the conversation. Most prefer "reliable net 30" over "promised net 15, actual net 22."

### Deferred subscriptions

Three months of expenses. Used in last 30 days → keep; 1–3 times in 90 days → downgrade; untouched in 90 days → cancel. A typical 10-person agency finds $1,500–$4,000/month here.

### Freeze non-essential hiring

In a crunch, you're not hiring. Pause open reqs. Candidates mid-interview: "We're pausing for 60 days. Can we revisit?" Cost of an early-tenure hire — recruiting, onboarding, ramp — is $30–60K in 90 days. That's runway.

## 5. Lever 3: Raise short-term capital

| Source | Cost (APR equivalent) | Speed | When it fits |
|--------|-----------------------|-------|--------------|
| Line of credit | 8–18% | 2–6 weeks | Predictable gap, established credit |
| Invoice factoring | 1.5–6%/invoice (effective 30–60% APR) | 3–7 days | A/R-heavy, weaker credit, urgent |
| Personal loan / HELOC | 7–15% | 1–3 weeks | Founder-bridge, small amounts |
| Equity (F&F / angel) | Permanent dilution | 30–90 days | Structural, not bridge |

**Line of credit.** Cheapest legitimate option. [Lines run single- to mid-teens, interest only on what you borrow](https://www.ondeck.com/resources/factoring-vs-line-of-credit). Open one *before* you need it. Applying during a crunch is too slow — banks take 2–6 weeks and will see your tight position.

**Invoice factoring.** [Fees 1.5–6% per invoice, monthly — effective 30–60% APR at 60–90-day client pay](https://www.uschamber.com/co/run/finance/invoice-factoring-vs-invoice-financing). Factor advances 80–90% within days. Fits when you can't get a line of credit and need cash this week. Anything past 60–90 days of factoring signals a structural problem.

**Personal loan / HELOC.** HELOC at 8% is cheaper than factoring. Both personally guaranteed — only if (a) the business problem is genuinely temporary and (b) the amount is small relative to your personal net worth. **Never** drain personal emergency funds.

**Equity.** Raising during a visible crunch is the most expensive capital you'll take. Valuations are punitive when you're a forced seller. Raise *before* the crunch is visible — or after you've stabilized.

## 6. The 60-day cash flow forecast

You can't manage what you can't see. Build a weekly 60-day forecast — the [SBA-recommended 13-week rolling model](https://www.prairiebookkeeping.com/resources/free-13-week-cash-flow-forecast-template), shortened. Columns per week, rows for:

```
Opening cash
+ A/R collections (known confirmed dates)
+ A/R collections (estimated, weighted by likelihood)
+ New revenue (signed contracts only)
+ Capital draws
- Payroll
- Contractors
- A/P (committed)
- Rent / SaaS / utilities
- Taxes
- Loan / line payments
= Ending cash
```

**Populate honestly.** A/R: confirmed-date at 100%, good clients 90%, stretch 50%, ghosting 0%. New revenue: only signed or decision-maker verbal commitments. A/P: include credit card bill, quarterly tax estimate, annual insurance renewal.

**Update weekly.** 60 minutes every Monday. Roll forward one week. Flag any week where ending cash drops below one payroll cycle.

This discipline converts cash flow from a crisis into a managed metric. The point isn't precision — it's spotting a shortfall 6 weeks out, when you can still solve it.

## 7. The conversation with the team

Your team will sense the crunch before you tell them — closed doors, slowed hiring, executive whispers. Hiding makes it worse — [employees consistently prefer the truth, even when hard, over rumor](https://www.sage.com/en-us/blog/small-business-financial-transparency/).

**When:** payroll risk within 60 days → tell now. 90-day pinch with high confidence in resolution → you can wait, but commit to an update date.

**How:** in person or all-hands video, not Slack. Specific, calm, with a plan.

### Script — all-hands transparency

> "Thanks for jumping on. I want to share where we are honestly, because you deserve to hear it from me first.
>
> Short version: revenue is solid, but we have a cash flow squeeze caused by [specific reason — late client payments / Q2 sales gap]. We are not in crisis. We are not laying anyone off. Payroll is funded for [specific date range].
>
> What I'm doing: [3 specific things — accelerating collections on top 5 A/R balances, pausing two open roles, vendor conversations on extended terms]. What I'm asking from you: [specific — flag client billing delays, hold off on non-critical purchases, keep doing great work].
>
> I'll give you an update every two weeks until this is behind us. Next update is [date]. My door is open, and so is [CFO/Ops lead]'s.
>
> Questions?"

Three rules: name the problem specifically, name the plan specifically, name the next update specifically. Vagueness on any breaks trust.

## 8. The conversation with critical vendors

### Script — calling a critical vendor

> "Hi [Name], thanks for taking the call. I want to be straight with you.
>
> We've been a customer for [X years], and our relationship matters. We're going through a 60-day cash flow squeeze — nothing existential, but real. I'm reaching out before our next invoice is due because I'd rather plan together than have you chasing me in 30 days.
>
> Can we move from net 15 to net 60 for the next three invoices — June, July, August? After that, back to standard. To make that work for you, I can [offer — pay 50% on time and 50% in 30 days / longer commitment / small late fee / wire instead of ACH].
>
> I'd much rather agree to this together than have you wonder if you're getting paid. Will that work?"

If no, ask what *would* work: "What's the most flexibility you can give, even if it's not what I asked?" Most vendors have more discretion than their AR rep admits.

Get the agreement **in writing** — email confirming is enough; signed addendum is better. [Without documentation, you've created a verbal modification that neither side will remember in 60 days](https://www.uschamber.com/co/run/business-financing/how-to-negotiate-payment-terms-with-vendors).

## 9. Common mistakes

1. **Not catching it early.** Looking at A/P aging 5 days too late, missing the moment to renegotiate proactively. The 60-day forecast solves this.
2. **Borrowing personally from emergency funds.** Draining personal savings for business payroll feels noble. It isn't. If the business fails anyway, you have neither business nor personal runway. Use credit, not cash.
3. **Ignoring collections out of guilt.** "I don't want to chase Client X — they're a great client." Great clients pay on time. A client 60 days overdue isn't being great to you.
4. **Mass layoffs as first move.** Layoffs are real money — severance, lost productivity, recruiting cost when you re-hire, damaged brand. Pull only after exhausting the first two levers. Hiring freezes and contractor wind-downs come first.
5. **Hiding from the team.** Closed-door calls, postponed all-hands, vague reassurances. Top performers start interviewing externally. Transparency with a plan beats silence.

## 10. When to bring in professional help

**Bookkeeper** ($400–1,500/month). Upgrade if you can't pull current A/R aging in 5 minutes, books are 30+ days behind, you can't articulate gross margin by service line, or you extended last year.

**Fractional CFO** ($3,000–8,000/month, 10–20 hours). Trigger: revenue above $1M with no full-time finance leader, recurring cash surprises, about to raise or take on debt, or you need a credible forecast and can't build one. Typically pays for itself in 90 days through collections, vendor renegotiations, and pricing discipline.

**Collections agency.** Trigger at 60+ days overdue, no payment plan, no responsiveness. Cost 25–50% contingency. Don't wait past 90 days.

**Bankruptcy / restructuring attorney.** The conversation no one wants, and why you should have it sooner. Triggers: net cash projection negative within 30 days with no bridge; personal guarantees exceeding liquid net worth; considering emergency capital you wouldn't take in a healthy moment. A one-hour consult is $300–600 and free of obligation. They'll tell you calmly what your options are — Chapter 11, assignment for benefit of creditors, wind-down, or "you're not as bad off as you think." Every founder who's had this conversation says: "I wish I'd done it three months ago." You are not failing if you make the call. You are doing your job.

---

## Final note

Cash crunches are not moral failings. They happen to good operators when one or two things outside your control land at once. Survivors aren't agencies that never had crunches — they're the ones that recognized early, diagnosed correctly, and had hard conversations on day 7 instead of day 70.

Diagnose. Build the 60-day forecast. Pull the cheapest lever first. Make the calls.
