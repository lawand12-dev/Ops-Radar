# Capacity planning — when to hire, outsource, or say no

Most sub-$10M agencies do not have a capacity problem. They have a *capacity-visibility* problem. The pipeline closes a deal, three people groan, the project gets shoved into next month, and quality dies a slow death over the next quarter. By the time the owner can see the crisis on a spreadsheet, the team is already burning out.

This playbook gives you the numbers and the decision rules to stay ahead of that curve. The math is boring on purpose — it works.

---

## 1. The four utilization thresholds

Utilization = billable hours ÷ available hours, measured per producer (designer, developer, copywriter, strategist), not per body in the building. Account managers, project managers, and ops staff do not count in producer utilization; track them on a separate dial.

Parakeeto's benchmark for **billable producer utilization is 65–80% on a weekly basis** and 65–80% on an annual basis depending on role seniority. ([Parakeeto utilization rate guide](https://www.parakeeto.com/blog/resource-utilization-capacity-planning-for-marketing-agency-2/)) Apply that range like a speed gauge:

| Band | What it means | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| **Under 60%** | Revenue problem. You are paying people to be available; the work isn't there. | Sales/marketing push. Look at lead gen, conversion, project mix. Do not hire. Consider letting attrition rebalance. |
| **60–75%** | Healthy. Producers can absorb a stretch, do training, and recover between projects. | Hold steady. Use the slack for skill upgrades, R&D, retros, internal tooling. |
| **75–85%** | Warning. Single-point-of-failure risk. Sickness, vacation, or one bad estimate tips you red. | Start recruiting *now*. Tighten estimates. Decline misfit work. Begin contractor onboarding before you need them. |
| **85%+** | Danger. Burnout, quality collapse, missed deadlines, voluntary attrition follow within 60–90 days. | Stop selling capacity-heavy work. Pull contractors in this week. Reset client delivery dates honestly. |

Two important caveats:

- **Producer roles run hotter than the average.** Parakeeto puts the weekly target for producers at 75–90% and the annual target at 65–80% — so a single producer touching 85% in one week is not a fire, but a *team* averaging 85% for three weeks is. ([Parakeeto utilization rate guide](https://www.parakeeto.com/blog/resource-utilization-capacity-planning-for-marketing-agency-2/))
- **The whole-agency annual net utilization target is lower — 50–60%** — because it includes non-billable roles. Do not mix the two numbers when reporting to yourself or to investors. ([Parakeeto](https://www.parakeeto.com/blog/resource-utilization-capacity-planning-for-marketing-agency-2/), [tmetric 2025 benchmarks](https://blog.tmetric.com/marketing-agency-profitability-benchmarks/))

## 2. Reading the signals 60–90 days out

Burnout doesn't show up in utilization first. It shows up in soft signals that the owner notices and the spreadsheet doesn't. Watch for these *together* — any single one is noise; three at once is a flashing light.

**Quantitative leading indicators:**

1. Producer utilization >80% for 3+ consecutive weeks.
2. Sold pipeline (signed SOWs not yet started) exceeds 4 weeks of available producer hours.
3. PTO denials or rescheduled PTO trending up.
4. Revision rounds per deliverable climbing (quality slipping under load).
5. Average project margin dropping while revenue is flat or rising (you're working more for the same money).

**Qualitative signals:**

1. Senior producers being pulled onto junior work because the junior is buried.
2. Slack/email response times to clients lengthening.
3. The owner or PM lead becoming the bottleneck — every escalation routes through them.
4. "Heroic" weekends starting to show up in the timesheet narrative.

Karl Sakas frames the owner's gut-check question well: *"Can we accomplish this work well without requiring heroic efforts? If it would take late nights or cut corners elsewhere, we'll pause or refer it out."* ([Sakas & Company office hours](https://sakasandcompany.com/agency-resource-planning-am-vs-pm-hiring-trends-profdev-incentives-agency-qa-with-karl-sakas/))

The high-utilization-plus-new-sales combination is the dangerous one. A 78% utilized team with three deals about to close on Friday is already over capacity — the math just hasn't caught up yet. Always evaluate utilization *against signed plus weighted pipeline*, not against booked work alone.

## 3. The decision tree: hire, contractor, or say no

Once you've decided you cannot deliver the next 60 days of demand with your current team and current pace, you have exactly three levers. Run any new demand through this tree.

**Question 1: Is this demand recurring or one-off?**

- *Recurring* (this skill will be needed every month for the foreseeable future, 15+ hours/week of work): go to Q2.
- *Spike* (project-shaped, 1–3 months, then probably gone): **use a contractor**. Stop here.

**Question 2: Is the role a generalist core skill or a specialist?**

- *Core skill you sell every day* (the production work that defines your service): go to Q3.
- *Specialist* (motion design, paid media analytics, accessibility audits, niche language localization): **use a contractor or fractional hire**. The math on a full-time specialist rarely works below ~$5M revenue.

**Question 3: Can you afford a fully-loaded hire and absorb 60–90 days of ramp?**

- *Yes*: **hire**. Go to section 4 to confirm the math.
- *No, or unsure*: **contractor bridge** — bring in a contractor at 25–40% markup now, hire FT in 90–180 days once the demand is proven and cash position improves.

**Question 4 (the override): Is this client/work actually a fit?**

If the demand triggering all this is from a misfit client — wrong industry, wrong scope, wrong size, perpetual scope creep — **say no.** Hiring or subcontracting to serve a bad fit is the most expensive capacity decision you can make. See section 6 for scripts.

Karl Sakas' threshold for an FT hire is **15–20 hours/week of confirmed billable work** for the role. Below that line, contractors win every time. ([Sakas, Build a Better Agency / AMI guidance](https://sakasandcompany.com/agency-resource-planning-am-vs-pm-hiring-trends-profdev-incentives-agency-qa-with-karl-sakas/))

## 4. Hiring math: fully-loaded cost and break-even

Base salary is the smallest part of what a hire actually costs you. Plan against the fully-loaded number or you will keep being surprised when the new hire doesn't make you more money.

**The 1.3–1.4× multiplier.** Across US-based agency hires, fully-loaded cost runs **1.25× to 1.45× base salary**, most commonly settled around 1.3×. ([Glen Coyne guide](https://www.glencoyne.com/guides/fully-loaded-cost-us-employee), [Scalearmy breakdown](https://scalearmy.com/blog/calculate-fully-loaded-cost-of-an-employee/)) Components:

- Employer FICA: 7.65% of wages.
- Health/dental/vision: ~$6,000–$15,000/yr depending on coverage and family status.
- 401(k) match: 3–4% of salary if competitive.
- Workers comp + state unemployment: 1–3% of wages.
- Paid time off, holidays, sick: ~10–12% effective cost (you're paying for non-working time).
- Equipment + software seats: $1,500–$3,000 upfront, $2,000–$5,000/yr ongoing. ([Glen Coyne](https://www.glencoyne.com/guides/fully-loaded-cost-us-employee))
- Recruiter fee, if used: 15–25% of first-year salary, one-time.
- **Management time**: a new producer typically absorbs 5–10 hours/week of a senior person's time for the first 90 days. At a $150/hr internal cost rate, that's $5,000–$10,000 of hidden ramp cost.

**Worked example — a $75,000 mid-level designer:**

| Line | Amount |
|---|---|
| Base salary | $75,000 |
| Fully-loaded at 1.3× | $97,500 |
| Equipment year 1 | $2,500 |
| Software seats year 1 | $3,000 |
| Recruiter (if used, mid) | $15,000 |
| Hidden management ramp (90 days) | $7,500 |
| **True year-1 cost** | **~$125,500** |
| **Year-2 steady-state cost** | **~$100,500** |

**Break-even on new billable revenue.** At a target of 75% producer utilization on a 40-hour week × 48 working weeks = **1,440 billable hours/year**. To clear year-1 fully-loaded cost on revenue alone (no margin):

- $125,500 ÷ 1,440 hrs = **~$87/hr effective billing rate just to break even**.

If your blended billing rate is $150/hr, that hire generates ~$216,000 in revenue and ~$90,000 in gross margin year 1 — which then has to cover overhead (Parakeeto pegs agency overhead at ~30% of AGI) before it contributes to net profit. ([Parakeeto metrics](https://www.parakeeto.com/blog/agency-metrics/))

**The brutal rule of thumb:** a producer hire should generate **3× their fully-loaded cost in revenue** to be a healthy contributor. Below 2.5×, the hire is dilutive to margin.

## 5. Contractor math: when contractors win

Contractors win in four scenarios:

1. **Spike demand** — a 6–10 week project, then nothing.
2. **Specialist skill** — you need it on three clients a year, not three days a week.
3. **Geographic or timezone coverage** — overnight QA, a regional language, weekend on-call.
4. **You can't afford the salary yet but the work is here now** — contractor bridges you across 90–180 days.

**Markup math.** The standard agency markup on a contractor's rate is **25–40%**, broadly aligned with general-contractor industry norms of 20–40% covering overhead, coordination, and risk. ([Angi: general contractor markup](https://www.angi.com/articles/general-contractor-markup.htm), [Contractor Growth Network guide](https://www.contractorgrowthnetwork.com/margin-and-markup-comparison/)) Within that band:

- **25% markup**: low-management contractor, long relationship, predictable scope. Mostly covering admin + a thin margin.
- **30–35% markup**: typical. Covers project management, QA, client communication, slight risk buffer.
- **40%+ markup**: high-coordination work, new contractor with limited track record, or specialist whose rate is already high (so absolute dollars are big even at high %).

**Worked example — a $90/hr contractor designer:**

| Line | Amount |
|---|---|
| Contractor's rate | $90/hr |
| Agency markup at 35% | $31.50/hr |
| **Billed to client at $121.50/hr** | $121.50/hr |
| Gross margin per billable hour | $31.50 (26% margin on revenue) |
| 200 hrs project → revenue | $24,300 |
| 200 hrs project → gross margin | $6,300 |

Compare against the FT hire: ~$15/hr more in gross margin per hour on FT *once steady state*, but zero downside risk and no fixed cost when work pauses on the contractor. Below ~600 hours/quarter of stable demand for the skill, contractors usually win on risk-adjusted basis. Above ~1,000 hours/quarter sustained, FT usually wins.

**Hidden contractor risk:** they ramp slower than expected on your tools, processes, and clients. Budget 10–15% of the engagement for onboarding loss in the first project — quote with that in mind.

## 6. Saying no without losing the relationship

Saying no is a capacity tool, not a sales failure. A well-handled no preserves the relationship, often gets you a referral back to that prospect later, and protects the team you already have.

Three usable templates — adapt voice, do not change structure:

**Script A — Push the start date (best when relationship matters and timing is flexible):**

> "Thanks for thinking of us on this. Looking honestly at our delivery calendar, we don't have the right team available to start this work before [date 8–10 weeks out]. If a [Month] kickoff works for you, I'd love to scope it properly now and hold the slot. If you need to move sooner, I'd rather tell you that up front than over-promise — happy to suggest someone strong if that's the case."

**Script B — Refer to a partner (best when timing is firm and you have a trusted referral partner):**

> "I appreciate you bringing this to us. We're at capacity through [quarter], and this project deserves a team that can give it real focus. [Partner name] at [partner agency] does excellent work in [specific niche relevant to the project] and I'd be glad to make a warm intro. Let me know if you'd like me to send their details, and feel free to tell them I referred you." ([Adapted from Really Good Designs scripts](https://reallygooddesigns.com/how-to-say-no-to-clients/))

**Script C — Narrow the scope (best when the prospect is great but the project is too big):**

> "I want to be straight with you. The full scope as outlined would stretch our team thinner than I'd be comfortable with, and the quality wouldn't be what we're known for. Here's what I'd propose: we take on [specific phase or workstream] now, deliver it well, and revisit the rest in [Q3] when we'd have the right team in place. That way you get real momentum on the highest-value piece and we don't burn either of us by overcommitting."

Two rules that make these scripts work:

1. **Never say no without offering a path** — a later start, a referral, a narrower scope, or an honest "this isn't our sweet spot." A bare no damages the brand; a no with a path strengthens it.
2. **Tell the truth about *why*.** "We're at capacity" is fine. "We're slammed" is not. Specificity reads as professionalism; vagueness reads as a dodge. Karl Sakas frames the principle as *not burning bridges* — you can decline cleanly without inventing a story. ([Sakas: how to say no without burning bridges](https://sakasandcompany.com/how-to-say-no-in-agency-client-service/))

## 7. The 6-month sales/capacity coupling

The biggest single mistake in agency hiring is reactive timing — hiring at 100% utilization, when the team is already drowning and the new hire takes 60–90 days to be productive. By the time they're useful, you've lost a producer to burnout.

The fix is a rolling 6-month coupling between sales pipeline and producer capacity. The math is simple:

```
Required new producer hours (next 90 days) =
  (weighted pipeline $ × historical win rate) ÷ blended billing rate
```

Then compare to:

```
Available producer hours (next 90 days) =
  (producers × 40 hrs × 12 weeks × target utilization %)
  − already-booked hours
```

If required > available, you have a 90-day capacity gap. Convert that gap into FTE-equivalents and start the hiring or contractor process now, not when the gap actually arrives.

**Worked example:**

- Pipeline: $450K weighted, win rate 35% → expected new revenue $157,500.
- Blended billing rate: $150/hr → required new hours = 1,050.
- Current producers: 6 × 40 hrs × 12 weeks × 75% target = 2,160 available hours.
- Already booked: 1,400 hours.
- Available net: 760 hours.
- **Gap: 1,050 needed − 760 available = 290 hour shortfall over 90 days, ≈ 1.0 FTE producer.**

Recommended action: start recruiting now, bridge first 60 days with contractors. Do not wait until producers are at 95% to act.

**Cadence rule:** review pipeline-vs-capacity **weekly**. Most agencies that get hit by capacity crises were reviewing it monthly or quarterly and missed the trend. Productive, Scoro, and Parakeeto all converge on weekly as the right cadence for sub-$10M agencies. ([Parakeeto forecasting guide](https://www.parakeeto.com/blog/a-quick-guide-to-digital-agency-forecasting/), [Scoro capacity planning](https://www.scoro.com/blog/capacity-planning/))

## 8. Common mistakes

Watch for these — each one is recoverable once, expensive twice, fatal three times.

1. **Hiring in firefighting mode.** You wait until the team is at 95%+, then panic-hire. You skip culture screening, skip skills tests, and onboard someone into chaos. Six months later you're either firing them or losing the senior who burned out babysitting them. Fix: adopt Karl Sakas' "Always Be Recruiting" — keep a warm pipeline of 2–3 candidates per critical role, even when you're not hiring. ([Sakas](https://sakasandcompany.com/agency-resource-planning-am-vs-pm-hiring-trends-profdev-incentives-agency-qa-with-karl-sakas/))

2. **Hiring too fast on optimistic pipeline.** You hire FTE on weighted pipeline of $400K assuming 50% win rate. You actually win 30%. Now you have a $125,000/yr commitment against the wrong revenue assumption and 6 months of cash runway pain. Fix: weight pipeline conservatively (use trailing-12-month actual win rate, not the rate you hope for), and bridge the first quarter with contractors.

3. **Promoting your best senior producer into management without role fit.** The best designer is rarely the best design director. You lose your top producer to a job they don't want and aren't suited for, and you usually lose them to a competitor 9 months later. Fix: write the management role spec independently of "who's senior," interview internally and externally for it, and offer dual tracks (IC senior vs. management) so individual contributors can keep growing without becoming managers.

4. **No role spec before recruiting.** "We need another developer" is not a role spec. Without a written spec (responsibilities, success metrics, skills, level, salary band) you'll interview to vibes, hire to vibes, and fire to vibes. Fix: a one-page role spec, written by the hiring manager, approved by ownership, before any recruiter is engaged or any candidate is contacted.

5. **Building the whole capacity plan around the wrong number.** Whole-agency utilization (50–60% healthy) and producer utilization (65–80% healthy) get confused. Owners report the lower number to themselves and feel underutilized when producers are already in the red. Fix: dashboard them separately, never blended. ([Parakeeto](https://www.parakeeto.com/blog/resource-utilization-capacity-planning-for-marketing-agency-2/))

6. **Treating contractor markup as profit.** It's not — most of it is overhead for managing the contractor (PM time, QA, client comms, payment terms, IP risk). If you treat the full 30% markup as drop-through margin, you'll under-price the next contractor-led project and lose money on it.

## 9. Subcontractor agreement essentials

Don't ship work to a contractor without these in writing. The cost of a bad agreement is almost always paid in lost IP, lost clients, or both.

**Must-haves:**

1. **IP assignment / work-made-for-hire clause.** Without explicit assignment, the contractor — not your agency, and not your client — owns the deliverable. ([Cooley GO: contractor agreements](https://www.cooleygo.com/absolutely-need-contractor-agreements/)) Spell it out: all work product, derivative works, and underlying assets are assigned to the agency upon payment.

2. **Confidentiality / NDA.** Specifically covers client identities, client data, business processes, pricing, and project details. Survives termination of the contract.

3. **Non-solicitation of clients and employees** (in place of a non-compete). Post-FTC ruling and given misclassification risk, broad non-competes are generally unenforceable and increase your audit exposure with contractors. ([Worksuite: independent contractor agreements](https://worksuite.com/resources/insights/independent-contractor-agreements), [Oyster on non-competes](https://www.oysterhr.com/library/non-compete-agreements-for-independent-contractors)) Use a **12–24 month non-solicit** instead: the contractor agrees not to directly solicit your named clients or other contractors/employees for their own benefit. This is enforceable in most US jurisdictions and accomplishes the real business goal.

4. **Payment terms.** Rate, invoice cadence, payment terms (net 15 or net 30), late-payment provisions. Match your client-side payment terms so you're not financing the contractor out of your own cash.

5. **Quality standards and acceptance.** Define what "done" looks like — file formats, revision rounds included, turnaround SLAs, definition of acceptable quality. Without this, "fix it" arguments become billable disputes.

6. **No-subcontracting clause.** The contractor cannot themselves subcontract the work to a third party without your written approval. Critical for IP and confidentiality chains. ([Time Doctor: outsourcing agreement essentials](https://www.timedoctor.com/blog/outsourcing-agreement/))

7. **Independent-contractor status acknowledgement.** Both parties confirm the relationship is not employment, contractor pays their own taxes, contractor is not entitled to benefits. Helps with classification audits.

8. **Termination for convenience + termination for cause.** Either party can end the engagement on 7–14 days notice without cause; immediate termination for material breach (confidentiality, IP, no-show on deliverables).

9. **Insurance and indemnification.** Contractor carries their own professional liability / general liability insurance (or you knowingly take the risk). Mutual indemnification for third-party claims arising from each party's work.

10. **Tools, data return, and offboarding.** On termination, contractor returns all client data, logs out of all systems, and confirms in writing that no copies are retained. Cover whose tools/seats are used during the engagement and how that's billed.

**One non-essential that's easy to over-rely on:** lengthy non-competes. They look protective and they're rarely enforceable on contractors. Spend the protection budget on tight IP assignment, tight non-solicit, and tight no-subcontracting clauses instead. ([Worksuite](https://worksuite.com/resources/insights/independent-contractor-agreements), [The Legal Paige](https://thelegalpaige.com/blogs/blog/why-you-cant-have-a-non-compete-clause-in-a-contractor-agreement))

## TL;DR — the owner's weekly 10-minute check

Every Monday, ask three questions:

1. **What's our producer utilization this week and last week?** If two weeks above 80%, start a capacity conversation.
2. **What's the weighted pipeline for the next 90 days, and how many producer hours does it imply?** If implied hours > available hours, decide hire vs. contractor this week.
3. **Is anything currently in pipeline a misfit?** If yes, plan the no — script A, B, or C — and use it this week, not next month.

Do this weekly and you will almost never face a capacity crisis. Skip it for a quarter and you will.
