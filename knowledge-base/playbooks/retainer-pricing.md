# Retainer pricing — when and how to raise your rates

If you run an agency on retainers, the highest-leverage move this year is probably not who you hire or what tool you buy — it's whether you reprice. Every section below ends with something you can do today.

## 1. The 4 signals it's time to reprice

Don't reprice on vibes. Reprice when at least one of these four signals is unambiguous.

### Signal 1: Delivery margin below 50%, net margin below 20%

Parakeeto's benchmark: a healthy agency runs **50%+ delivery margin** (AGI minus delivery cost, over AGI) and **20%+ net margin**, with high performers near 30% net. Industry average is below 15% — inadequate.

Per retainer:

- **Delivery margin** = (Monthly fee − Direct delivery cost) ÷ Monthly fee
- **Direct delivery cost** = Hours logged × fully-loaded cost per hour

A $5K/month retainer consuming 40 hours at $90 fully-loaded = $3,600 cost on $5,000 fee = 28% delivery margin. That's bleeding — either delivery is broken, scope expanded, or you under-priced. (Source: Parakeeto's [profitability guide](https://parakeeto.com/blog/measuring-and-improving-your-agencys-profitabilty-the-2023-guide/) and [rate calculator](https://www.parakeeto.com/blog/agency-rate-calculator-determine-your-hourly-rate/).)

**Action:** Pull last 3 months of hours per retainer. Compute delivery margin. Flag everything below 50%.

### Signal 2: You haven't raised this client in 18+ months

Karl Sakas recommends repricing **every 1–2 years**. Patrick Campbell of ProfitWell argues for revisiting pricing every quarter. Costs rise ~5% a year — three years of silence = ~15 margin points gone. That's the gap between healthy and underwater.

**Action:** Log the last rate change for every retainer. Anything past 18 months goes on the list.

### Signal 3: Scope has drifted since the last rate

The silent killer. Same dollar amount, but now you're also producing the monthly report, attending a second standup, fielding weekend Slacks, or absorbing a "small" extra channel. Parakeeto's data shows retainers *should* hit 40–55% net effective margin, but scope creep collapses them to project-tier margins or worse.

**Action:** For each retainer, list (a) the signed scope vs. (b) what was actually delivered last quarter. If the actual list is materially longer, reprice is justified.

### Signal 4: Your new-client price is materially higher

Quoting new clients at $7,500/month for work that legacy clients pay $5,000 for is a pricing-arbitrage problem. Karl Sakas's move: raise public/new-client pricing first, then use the gap as the anchor for legacy repricing ("our new clients are now paying X% more for a retainer like yours").

**Action:** Compare median new-client price (last 6 months) vs. median legacy retainer price. Gap >15% → every legacy retainer is a candidate.

## 2. How to calculate the right new rate

Parakeeto's formula is the cleanest starting point:

**Agency Rate = AGI ÷ Delivery Hours**

Where AGI is revenue minus pass-throughs, and Delivery Hours is everything billed to the engagement (strategy, design, PM, execution). The target is at least **2.5× your Average Cost Per Hour (ACPH)** — meaning you spend no more than 40 cents to earn a dollar of revenue.

### Worked example

Your agency:

- Fully-loaded ACPH (salary + benefits + overhead allocation ÷ working hours): **$85/hour**
- Target margin: ACPH × 2.5 = **$212.50/hour billable rate**

Retainer client "Acme":

- Average monthly hours actually delivered (trailing 3 months): **42**
- Current monthly fee: **$5,000**
- Effective rate: $5,000 ÷ 42 = **$119/hour** (far below the $212.50 target)

To hit a 60% delivery margin at 42 hours of delivery:

- Required AGI = 42 × $212.50 = **$8,925/month**

So the math says raise this retainer to **~$8,900/month** to be on-target. That's a 78% jump and you likely won't land it in one move. Real-world repricings on legacy clients land in the **10–25%** range per cycle (Karl Sakas observed 93% acceptance at 10–25% in one content agency case study, with select clients accepting up to 50%).

**Practical sequencing for Acme:** Cycle 1 $5,000 → $6,000 (+20%). Cycle 2 (12 months later) → $7,200. Cycle 3 → $8,640. You converge on target across three cycles, not one.

**Action:** For each underwater retainer, compute (a) the target on-margin price, (b) a 15–25% increment for this cycle. Don't try to fix three years of under-pricing in one conversation.

## 3. The pricing conversation script

Three scripts, by client type. Karl Sakas's strong recommendation: **deliver this on a phone or video call, not in writing.** Email is for the follow-up confirmation. The call comes first, ideally at the end of an existing standing meeting, with ~2 minutes of script and deliberate pauses.

### Script A — The long-term client (3+ years, healthy relationship)

*[End of standing call. You speak last.]*

> "Before we wrap, I want to give you a heads-up about pricing. You've been with us at $5,000/month since [year]. Our new clients are now signing at $7,500 for the same retainer scope. Because of the relationship and the results we've built together, we're not putting you at new-client pricing. Starting [date 90 days out], your retainer will move to $6,000/month — that's a 20% adjustment, well below the market rate.
>
> Nothing else changes — same team, same scope, same cadence. I wanted to tell you directly before you saw it on an invoice. Any questions?"

Why this works: anchors to new-client price (which is higher), positions the increase as a *discount* off market, fixes a date, leaves space for the client to ask questions. This is Karl Sakas's "happy client" pattern almost verbatim.

### Script B — The recent client (6–18 months in, mid-engagement)

You're on shakier ground here — they haven't seen enough cycles to internalize your value. Give them an option.

*[On a call.]*

> "I want to flag something on pricing. We're moving our retainer rates for [year] and your engagement is included. Starting [date 90 days out], your retainer will be $6,000/month — up from $5,000.
>
> Two options: if you'd like to lock in the current $5,000 for another 12 months, sign a new annual retainer in the next 30 days and we'll honor it. Otherwise the rate moves to $6,000 month-to-month. Either is fine on our side. Which works better for you?"

This is Karl Sakas's "neutral client" frame: lock-in trade for a 12-month commitment, or the increase. You get either a longer contract or a higher rate. Both improve agency LTV.

### Script C — The struggling client (margin already underwater, behavior issues, or budget-fragile)

Reason → Options → Choose. Be honest, not apologetic.

*[On a call. No small talk before — get to it.]*

> "I want to be straight with you about where we are. Over the last six months, the scope of what we deliver for you has expanded — [name one or two specific things, e.g., 'we added the second weekly report and the LinkedIn channel']. At the current $5,000, the math doesn't work for our team to keep doing it well.
>
> I see three paths forward. One: we move the retainer to $6,500/month starting [date] and keep the current scope. Two: we hold at $5,000 and we tighten back to the original scope — [list what gets cut]. Three: if neither of those fits your budget right now, we can put together a transition plan and help you hand off to another partner. I want to do the right thing for both sides. Which of those feels closest?"

Option three is the unlock. Naming the exit gives the client psychological space to actually engage with options one and two. This is closely aligned with Karl Sakas's "difficult/low-budget" framework.

**Action:** Map every reprice candidate to Script A, B, or C. Book the calls. Don't email.

## 4. Handling objections

Pre-write your responses. Improvising costs you margin.

### "Our budget is locked for the year."

> "I understand. The new rate takes effect [date], which gives you [X] months to plan it into next cycle. To make it easier, I can hold the current rate through [end of their budget year] if you commit to the new rate from [start of next year] — would that work?"

Sandler-style reframing: acknowledge the constraint, offer a path that respects it without dropping the new rate.

### "We'll churn / we'll have to cancel."

> "I hear you, and I don't want to lose the engagement. Before we go there — what's the part of what we do that's hardest to give up? Let's talk about whether there's a tighter scope that fits the current budget, because we'd rather keep working together at a sustainable shape than end it."

You're collaborative, but you don't drop the rate. You drop *scope*. Per HubSpot's price-objection playbook, the move is to reduce delivered value to match price, not to discount price at the same value.

### "We'll find someone cheaper."

> "You probably can. The reason we're at this rate is [one specific, verifiable result — revenue, retention, pipeline]. If a cheaper provider can deliver that same outcome, that's a fair switch. If you want, I can put together a transition plan to make the handoff clean."

Calling the bluff *with* a clean offramp removes the threat. Most clients back down. The ones that don't were unprofitable anyway.

### "This isn't a good time."

> "Totally fair. The new rate is locked for [date]. There won't ever be a perfect time — but I wanted you to hear it from me three months out instead of finding it on an invoice. Let's stay the course and revisit in [60 days] if anything's changed on your end."

You're not asking permission. You're delivering information with notice. Don't get drawn into renegotiating timing under emotional pressure.

**Action:** Print these four lines and have them open during every reprice call.

## 5. Forecasting impact

The math is unforgiving in your favor. A small rate move across a retainer book compounds fast.

**Formula:** Annualized revenue gain = (Average retainer × Rate increase %) × Number of clients × 12

### Worked example: 12 clients at $5,000/month

| Rate bump | Per-client monthly gain | Per-client annual gain | Total annual gain (12 clients) |
|-----------|-------------------------|------------------------|--------------------------------|
| 5%        | $250                    | $3,000                 | **$36,000**                    |
| 10%       | $500                    | $6,000                 | **$72,000**                    |
| 15%       | $750                    | $9,000                 | **$108,000**                   |
| 20%       | $1,000                  | $12,000                | **$144,000**                   |
| 25%       | $1,250                  | $15,000                | **$180,000**                   |

Two things to internalize:

1. **The increase is almost pure margin.** You're not adding scope, hiring, or buying software — delivery cost is fixed, the rate bump drops to the bottom line. A 10% bump on this book = $72K of nearly-100%-margin revenue.

2. **Even with churn, the math still works.** At 10% bump with 10% churn: 10.8 × $5,500 × 12 = $712,800 vs. baseline $720K — basically flat on revenue, up on profit per hour. At 15% bump with 10% churn: **net positive** by $26K. The clients you lose are usually the underwater ones.

**Action:** Build this table for your own book. Pin the 10% and 15% scenarios on your wall. They are the case for action.

## 6. Common mistakes

Six failure modes, ranked by how often they sink a reprice cycle.

### 1. Apologizing in the email

"We regret to inform you that, unfortunately, we are forced to…" — every word of that is wrong. You're not regretful, no one is forcing you, and "unfortunately" cues the client that this is bad news worth fighting. Per invoicequick.com's [communication guide](https://invoicequick.com/blog/how-to-inform-customers-of-a-price-increase-2025-guide-with-templates-email-scripts), the rule is: own the decision, frame it as sustaining the quality of work. "Our retainer rates are moving on [date]" is a sentence, not an apology.

### 2. Offering a discount before they ask

If you walk into the call already prepared to say "but we can do 8% instead of 15% if it's a problem," you'll say it. And the client — who would have accepted 15% — will negotiate you down. Hold your number. Let *them* raise the objection. Then use the scripts above.

### 3. Raising every client at the same time

Sakas and Shopify's [agency pricing guide](https://www.shopify.com/partners/blog/raise-prices-agency) both recommend a tiered rollout: start with a small group (your healthiest, happiest clients), refine the script based on their reactions, then expand. Repricing all 12 clients on the same Monday means if your messaging is off, you find out 12 times.

### 4. Raising mid-engagement without a notice period

A retainer is a recurring contract. Changing the price without ≥90 days' notice on retainers (30 days minimum for projects) violates the implicit covenant of the relationship and gives the client legitimate grounds to walk. Invoicequick.com and most agency advisors converge on **30–90 days advance notice**, with the longer end for retainers.

### 5. Hiding the new rate

Don't bury the number. The client should know, in the first 30 seconds of the call or the first paragraph of the email: the *old* rate, the *new* rate, and the *effective date*. Vagueness reads as evasion and erodes trust.

### 6. Repricing while you're underperforming

If you missed a deadline last week or there's an open complaint — fix it *first*, then reprice 60–90 days later. Patrick Campbell's rule applies: data might justify a 50% increase, but with recent service degradation, you wait two months and move 30% instead.

## 7. When NOT to raise

Skip a reprice cycle on a specific client if any of these are true.

- **Within the first 6 months of the retainer.** They haven't seen a full cycle of your work yet. There's no reference frame for the value. Wait one full year minimum on new retainers.
- **During an unresolved delivery issue.** A missed deadline, a quality dispute, or a complaint within the last 60 days. Resolve, give it 60 days of clean delivery, then reprice.
- **During onboarding friction.** If you're still in the awkward phase of learning their stack, voice, or stakeholders, a price bump will land as opportunistic. Get them to "settled" first.
- **During a known crisis at their business.** Layoffs, funding fall-through, lost major customer. Repricing into someone else's crisis is reputationally expensive and they'll remember it for years.
- **Inside the same calendar quarter as a contract renewal.** Pick one moment, not two. Either reprice *at* renewal or 90 days before, not both.

These are deferrals, not exemptions. Note the reason, set a 60- or 90-day reminder, and revisit.

## 8. The 30/60/90 sequence

The mechanical execution after the decision is made.

### T-90: Announce (the call)

- Calendar a 15-minute call with the client lead. If you have a standing weekly meeting, append it there.
- Use Script A, B, or C depending on client type.
- Confirm the new rate, the effective date, and that nothing else changes (or what changes, if scope is being adjusted).
- **Same day:** send a confirmation email. Subject: "Following up on our call — retainer adjustment effective [date]". Body restates old rate, new rate, effective date, and one line of "looking forward to continuing the work." No apologies, no hedging.

### T-60: Confirm

- Reference the announcement inside an existing project communication (status update, monthly report). One line: "Quick reminder — the retainer adjustment we discussed takes effect [date]. Your [Month] invoice will reflect the new rate of $X. Let me know if any questions."
- If you haven't heard a peep, don't poke the bear. Silence at T-60 is acceptance.
- If they're raising fresh objections at T-60, use the objection scripts from Section 4. Do not move the date.

### T-30: Operational prep

- Update the retainer record in your billing system. Update the contract or order form if your relationship has one.
- Send an invoice preview: "Heads up — your next invoice on [date] will be at the new rate of $X. Just so it's not a surprise."
- Internal: brief the account/delivery team so no one on your side accidentally promises the old rate.

### T-0: Invoice at the new rate

- First invoice goes out at the new rate. Include a clean line item showing the rate and period. No commentary in the invoice itself.
- The day after the invoice goes out, send one short follow-up: "Your [Month] invoice landed at the new rate we discussed. Thanks for your continued partnership — and let me know if anything needs attention on our end."
- If they pay on time at the new rate, you're done. Calendar the next reprice review for 12–18 months out.

**Action:** Make this a template in your project management tool. Six clients × four touchpoints = 24 tasks. Schedule them all the day you make the reprice decision.

Now go look at your top three underwater retainers and book the calls. The math doesn't move until you do.
